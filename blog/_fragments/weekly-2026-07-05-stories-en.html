Story 1: PHL Variable Insurance Liquidation Pushed to 2027 as NOLHGA RFP Process Begins

Source: InsuranceNewsNet | Published: July 1, 2026

PHL Variable Insurance Co., the Connecticut-based life insurer that was placed into rehabilitation in May 2024 following the discovery of a massive capital deficit, will not be liquidated until at least 2027, state regulators confirmed this week. The announcement marks a significant extension from earlier projections that had anticipated a liquidation order by the end of 2026, and it underscores the extraordinary complexity of unwinding a major life insurance carrier while protecting hundreds of thousands of policyholders.

The company's financial deterioration has been severe and accelerating. When Connecticut's Insurance Commissioner first placed PHL Variable into rehabilitation two years ago, the capital shortfall stood at approximately $900 million. By the close of 2025, that deficit had ballooned to $2.2 billion, reflecting the compounding challenges of managing a distressed book of business in a volatile interest rate environment. The company's captive subsidiaries, Concord Re, Inc. and Palisado Re, Inc., are also part of the receivership proceedings.

The National Organization of Life and Health Insurance Guaranty Associations (NOLHGA) is now at the center of the transition plan. NOLHGA is managing a formal Request-for-Proposals (RFP) process expected to launch in Q3 2026, soliciting bids from solvent insurers willing to assume PHL's guaranteed policy obligations. The goal is to secure a transaction providing policyholders with benefits exceeding standard guaranty association caps.

Standard guaranty association coverage caps — typically $300,000 in death benefits and $250,000 in cash surrender value per policyholder, though limits vary by state — may not be sufficient to make whole all of PHL's policyholders. By using remaining estate assets to fund a transaction above those caps, regulators hope to reduce the financial harm to affected individuals and families.

As of June 23, 2026, approximately 40% of eligible policyholders had submitted their elections under a court-approved modification process. Processing for fixed indexed annuity holders had reached roughly 80% completion, while universal life processing was at approximately 60% completion. A moratorium on certain benefit payments and transactions remains in effect.

The timeline extension has significant implications for the approximately 40,000 to 50,000 estimated policyholders still in the rehabilitation. Many of these individuals are seniors who purchased variable annuities or universal life policies as part of their retirement and estate planning strategies. Some reports note potential losses exceeding $120 million for certain groups.

For the broader insurance industry, the PHL Variable case is a cautionary tale about the risks of offshore reinsurance structures and private credit exposure. Regulators have noted that the company's financial distress was partly attributable to complex reinsurance arrangements that obscured the true risk profile of its balance sheet, prompting intensified scrutiny through Actuarial Guideline 55 (AG 55).

The case also highlights the critical role of state guaranty associations in the U.S. insurance regulatory framework. Unlike bank deposits, which are federally insured by the FDIC up to $250,000, life insurance policies are protected by a patchwork of state-level guaranty funds. The adequacy of these protections is now under renewed scrutiny by policymakers and consumer advocates.

From a market perspective, the PHL Variable situation has reinforced demand for financially strong, highly rated carriers. Independent agents and brokers have reported increased client inquiries about carrier financial strength ratings from AM Best, Moody's, and S&P Global, particularly in the final expense and senior life insurance markets.

The NOLHGA RFP process is expected to attract interest from several large life insurance groups with the financial capacity to absorb a block of business of this size. However, the complexity of PHL's product mix — variable annuities, fixed indexed annuities, and universal life policies — may limit the pool of qualified bidders. Regulators will prioritize bids offering the highest level of policyholder protection.

★ What This Means for Agents

  • Carrier financial strength matters: when recommending life insurance or annuity products to seniors and final expense clients, proactively discuss AM Best ratings, surplus levels, and state guaranty association protections.
  • PHL policyholders need guidance: clients who hold PHL Variable policies should contact the Connecticut Insurance Department's receivership office for the latest information on their specific policy status.
  • Replacement requires care: be prepared to help affected clients explore replacement coverage options, evaluating suitability and potential surrender charges carefully.
  • A cautionary industry case: the PHL situation reinforces why clients increasingly ask about carrier financial strength ratings from AM Best, Moody's, and S&P Global.

Story 2: House Committee Unanimously Advances Clarity for Compensation Act for Financial Advisors

Source: InvestmentNews | Published: July 1, 2026

In a rare display of bipartisan unity, the U.S. House Financial Services Committee voted 51-0 on June 30, 2026, to advance the Clarity for Compensation Act (H.R. 7187), a bill that would fundamentally modernize how independent financial advisors and insurance professionals can receive compensation. The unanimous vote signals strong congressional support for removing a long-standing regulatory barrier that has complicated business operations for thousands of independent agents and registered representatives across the country.

The core issue the legislation addresses is deceptively simple: under current securities regulations, registered representatives — including many dual-licensed insurance and investment advisors — are required to receive commission payments directly as individuals rather than through their own business entities. This restriction creates significant operational and tax inefficiencies that do not apply to other licensed professionals such as attorneys, accountants, or insurance-only agents.

The Clarity for Compensation Act would change this by explicitly permitting registered representatives to receive compensation through personal services business entities — such as S-corporations or LLCs — that they own and control. For independent advisors who operate as small business owners, the ability to receive income through a business entity enables more efficient tax planning, facilitates the purchase of business-related benefits, and creates a cleaner structure for succession planning and business valuation.

NAIFA President Christopher Gandy emphasized that the bill recognizes the reality of how independent advisors actually operate: as entrepreneurs running small businesses, not as employees of the firms whose products they distribute. The Financial Services Institute (FSI), Finseca, and the Association of African American Financial Advisors (Quad-A) have also expressed strong support, noting that the measure would reduce operational inefficiencies and support succession planning for independent advisors.

The bill now awaits scheduling for a floor vote by the full House of Representatives. If it passes the House, it would then move to the Senate, where it would need to clear the Banking, Housing, and Urban Affairs Committee before reaching a floor vote. While the unanimous committee vote is an encouraging sign, the legislative calendar in the second half of 2026 is crowded, and there is no guarantee the bill will reach the President's desk before the end of the current Congress.

For the life insurance distribution channel specifically, the Clarity for Compensation Act addresses a structural inequity that has existed for decades. Insurance-only agents have always been able to receive commissions through their business entities — a flexibility that has supported the growth of independent marketing organizations (IMOs) and field marketing organizations (FMOs). Dual-licensed advisors who also hold securities registrations have been caught in a regulatory gap that the new legislation would close.

The timing of the bill's advancement is significant. The life insurance industry is in the midst of a major distribution transformation, with broker consolidation accelerating and independent advisors facing increasing pressure from both direct-to-consumer digital platforms and large captive agency systems. Legislation that reduces the operational burden on independent advisors and makes it easier to build and transfer a book of business could help sustain the independent distribution channel that accounts for the majority of life insurance sales.

★ What This Means for Agents

  • Consult compliance and tax advisors: if the Clarity for Compensation Act becomes law, dual-licensed agents should understand how the new rules apply to their specific business structure.
  • Potential tax advantages: receiving commissions through an LLC or S-corp could offer meaningful tax benefits, particularly for high-producing agents in upper income brackets.
  • Succession planning impact: agents planning to sell their practice or bring on a junior partner should explore how the new compensation structure could affect business valuation and transition planning.
  • Stay engaged with industry groups: follow updates from NAIFA and your broker-dealer as the bill moves through the legislative process.

Story 3: GLP-1 Drugs Reshaping Life Insurance Underwriting: Carriers Add Back Weight to Combat Mortality Slippage

Source: New Horizons Marketing | Published: July 1, 2026

The explosive growth of GLP-1 receptor agonist medications — including Ozempic (semaglutide), Wegovy, and Zepbound — has created a new and complex challenge for life insurance underwriters: how to accurately assess the mortality risk of applicants whose health metrics have been dramatically altered by drugs they may not continue taking. As of mid-2026, carriers across the industry are actively updating their underwriting guidelines, application forms, and risk assessment models to account for what actuaries are calling the "Ozempic effect."

The core problem is straightforward but actuarially significant. GLP-1 medications can produce dramatic improvements in health metrics over a relatively short period — reducing body weight by 15% to 20%, lowering blood pressure, improving HbA1c levels, and reducing cardiovascular risk markers. An applicant who has been on a GLP-1 for six months may present a health profile that looks substantially better than their underlying long-term risk profile, particularly if they discontinue the medication.

And discontinuation is common. Research consistently shows that a majority of GLP-1 users stop taking the medication within 12 months, often due to cost, side effects, or insurance coverage changes. When patients discontinue GLP-1 therapy, most experience significant weight regain — often recovering 60% to 70% of the weight lost within a year. This creates "mortality slippage": a situation where an insurer locks in a premium based on a temporarily improved health profile, only to have the applicant's actual risk revert to a higher level after policy issuance.

To combat this, life insurance underwriters are now implementing a range of adjustments. The most common approach is to add back approximately 50% of the weight lost in the prior 12 months when calculating BMI for underwriting purposes. So an applicant who weighed 250 pounds, lost 40 pounds on a GLP-1, and now weighs 210 pounds might be underwritten at a BMI based on a weight of 230 pounds rather than 210 pounds.

Application forms are also being updated. Until recently, most life insurance applications did not specifically ask about GLP-1 medication use. That is changing rapidly. Carriers are now adding explicit questions about weight-loss medication use, duration of use, and current dosage to their applications. Failure to disclose GLP-1 use when asked could constitute material misrepresentation, potentially voiding a policy at claim time.

Prescription database checks are becoming more rigorous as well. Carriers routinely access prescription history databases as part of the underwriting process, and GLP-1 prescriptions are now flagged for additional review. Some carriers are also exploring partnerships with pharmacy benefit managers to monitor medication adherence on an ongoing basis.

The Medicare GLP-1 Bridge program, which launched on July 1, 2026, adds another dimension to this issue. The program provides access to certain weight-loss GLP-1 medications for eligible Medicare Part D beneficiaries at a $50 monthly copay — a significant reduction from retail prices that can exceed $1,000 per month. This is expected to substantially increase GLP-1 use among the senior population, which is also the primary market for final expense and senior life insurance products.

For final expense agents specifically, the GLP-1 underwriting issue is particularly relevant. Many final expense clients are seniors with multiple health conditions, and GLP-1 medications are increasingly being prescribed not just for weight loss but for cardiovascular risk reduction and diabetes management. An agent who does not understand how a client's GLP-1 use will affect underwriting may inadvertently place a client with a carrier whose guidelines will result in a rated policy or a decline.

The actuarial uncertainty around GLP-1s extends beyond underwriting to product pricing. Carriers are grappling with how to incorporate GLP-1 data into their mortality tables and pricing models. If GLP-1 medications prove to have sustained long-term mortality benefits, carriers that are too conservative in their underwriting may find themselves at a competitive disadvantage. Regulators are watching this space closely, with the NAIC's Life Actuarial Task Force beginning discussions on how GLP-1 medications should be treated in actuarial assumptions.

★ What This Means for Agents

  • Ask about GLP-1 use upfront: before submitting an application, ask clients directly whether they are taking weight-loss or diabetes medications, including Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, or Zepbound.
  • Shop across carriers: if a client is on a GLP-1, compare underwriting guidelines across multiple carriers — the difference can be significant between preferred and substandard rates.
  • Document conversations carefully: ensure clients understand the importance of full disclosure on their applications to avoid material misrepresentation at claim time.
  • Final expense market impact: GLP-1 medications are now a routine part of the underwriting conversation for many senior and final expense clients.

Story 4: Corebridge Financial Enhances Max Accumulator+ III IUL with Nasdaq-100 and S&P 500 High Bonus Strategies

Source: Corebridge Financial Investor Relations | Published: June 29, 2026

Corebridge Financial, one of the largest life insurance and retirement services companies in the United States, announced on June 29, 2026, significant enhancements to its Max Accumulator+ III indexed universal life (IUL) insurance product. The updates add two new index crediting strategies — the Nasdaq-100 and the S&P 500 High Bonus — bringing the total number of available index strategies to five and substantially expanding the product's cash value accumulation potential for policyholders with longer time horizons and higher risk tolerance.

The Max Accumulator+ III is an accumulation-focused IUL product designed primarily for clients who want permanent life insurance protection combined with the opportunity to build tax-advantaged cash value linked to market index performance. Like all IUL products, it offers a floor that protects policy value from market losses — meaning that in years when the linked index performs negatively, the policy's credited interest rate does not fall below zero.

The newly added Nasdaq-100 strategy gives policyholders exposure to the performance of the technology-heavy Nasdaq-100 index, which tracks 100 of the largest non-financial companies listed on the Nasdaq Stock Market. For clients with a long time horizon — typically 15 to 20 years or more — the Nasdaq-100 strategy offers the potential for above-average cash value accumulation, subject to the product's participation rates and caps.

The S&P 500 High Bonus strategy incorporates a bonus mechanism that provides enhanced crediting in certain market scenarios. This type of strategy is particularly attractive in environments where standard S&P 500 caps are relatively low due to the cost of options, as the bonus feature can provide additional upside that partially compensates for cap limitations.

The product continues to offer its existing index strategies alongside the two new additions, giving policyholders and their advisors a menu of five options to allocate premium among. This diversification capability allows clients to spread their index exposure across different market segments and strategy types, potentially smoothing out the volatility of any single index's performance over time.

Corebridge's Agile Underwriting+ (AU+) program remains available for eligible applicants, offering the possibility of policy approval without a medical exam, lab work, or attending physician statement. The product is available for issue ages 0 to 80, with a minimum face amount of $50,000. Optional living benefit riders include a guaranteed lifetime income rider, a chronic illness/care expense rider, and overloan protection.

The product enhancements come at a time when IUL is one of the strongest-performing segments of the life insurance market. According to LIMRA's Q1 2026 data, IUL new annualized premium reached $1.1 billion in the first quarter, a 9% increase year-over-year, representing 25% of the total individual life insurance market. The growth is being driven by middle-income and mass-affluent buyers attracted to IUL's combination of permanent protection, tax-advantaged accumulation, and downside protection.

Corebridge's broader strategic context is also worth noting. The company is in the process of a planned merger with Equitable Holdings, expected to conclude by the end of 2026. S&P Global has assigned a "negative" outlook to Corebridge's ratings in connection with the merger, reflecting execution risks associated with integrating two large financial services organizations. However, the company has continued to invest in product development and distribution expansion, including a partnership with Allstate Financial Services.

For independent agents and IMOs, the Corebridge product enhancements represent an opportunity to revisit IUL conversations with clients who may have been on the fence about the product category. The addition of the Nasdaq-100 strategy in particular may appeal to younger clients — those in their 30s and 40s — who are comfortable with technology sector exposure and are looking for a life insurance product that can serve as a long-term wealth accumulation vehicle alongside their other investments.

★ What This Means for Agents

  • Stronger IUL story: the Max Accumulator+ III enhancements give agents a stronger narrative in IUL conversations, particularly for clients seeking technology sector growth or enhanced accumulation potential.
  • Use official illustrations: present realistic projections under multiple scenarios — including both the current illustrated rate and a more conservative stress-tested rate.
  • Explain crediting mechanics: ensure clients understand how caps, participation rates, and floors work, and how the new Nasdaq-100 and S&P 500 High Bonus strategies differ from standard S&P 500 strategies.
  • Full cost disclosure: disclose all product costs, including cost of insurance charges and administrative fees, for suitability compliance.
