Story 1: Industry Experts Warn NAIC: Fix Flawed IUL Illustrations Now

Source: InsuranceNewsNet | Published: June 18, 2026

The National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) is facing mounting pressure to overhaul how Indexed Universal Life (IUL) insurance policies are illustrated to consumers, with industry veterans warning that the current system is fundamentally misleading and generating a wave of litigation across the country. The alarm was sounded during a June 2026 meeting of the NAIC's Life Insurance and Annuities Committee, where Dick Weber — a 59-year veteran of the life insurance industry and representative of the Life Insurance Consumer Advocacy Center — delivered a pointed critique of existing illustration standards.

Weber was unequivocal in his assessment: the problem is not the IUL product itself, but the way it is illustrated. "The issue is not the product," he told regulators. "It's the illustration of IUL that is creating issues for us." He cited a rising tide of lawsuits involving IUL policies that failed to perform as illustrated, leaving policyholders — many of them retirees — with lapsed policies and unexpected tax consequences at the worst possible time in their financial lives.

At the heart of the problem is how IUL illustrations depict future crediting rates. Under current actuarial guidelines — specifically Actuarial Guideline 49-A (AG 49-A) — illustrations are permitted to show a constant crediting rate over the life of the policy, often spanning 30 to 40 years. This creates the impression of predictable, steady growth when in reality, IUL crediting rates are tied to market index performance and can vary dramatically from year to year based on caps, floors, and participation rates.

Weber presented a concrete hypothetical to illustrate the danger: a 45-year-old client contributes $25,000 annually for 20 years, with the illustration projecting $89,000 in annual tax-free retirement withdrawals based on a 6.55% internal rate of return. The illustration looks compelling on paper. But when Weber ran 1,000 stochastic simulations using actual historical market data, only 10% of scenarios showed the policy sustaining performance through age 100. In other words, 90% of the time, the policy would fail before the insured reached 100 — a catastrophic outcome for someone counting on that income in retirement.

The sensitivity of these projections to even small changes in assumptions is alarming. When Weber reduced the illustrated cap rate by just 1% — from 10.5% to 9.5% — the policy success rate in his simulations plummeted from 10% to just 1%. That means 989 out of 1,000 simulated scenarios resulted in policy failure. This extreme sensitivity is rarely, if ever, disclosed in standard IUL sales illustrations, leaving consumers with a dangerously incomplete picture of the risk they are accepting.

Weber also criticized the lack of transparency around IUL policy expenses. "IUL is almost always front-loaded with expenses," he noted, pointing out that the cost structure of these policies — including cost of insurance charges, administrative fees, and surrender charges — is rarely made clear in illustrations. In one legal case he referenced, a registered investment adviser recommended a premium-financed IUL strategy with long-term withdrawal projections. Stochastic analysis later revealed the policy was likely to lapse before the insured's life expectancy, triggering significant tax consequences for the client.

To address these shortcomings, Weber proposed two key reforms. First, he called for requiring or at minimum allowing stochastic analysis in IUL illustrations — a methodology that runs hundreds or thousands of simulations based on historical market data to show consumers a realistic range of possible outcomes rather than a single optimistic projection. Second, he advocated for replacing paper-based illustrations with interactive digital tools that allow consumers and agents to test how changes in crediting rates, caps, participation rates, and policy terms affect long-term performance.

Weber also noted that the NAIC's current Life Insurance Illustrations Model Regulation (#582) was developed in 1995 — before widespread internet access — and has not kept pace with the complexity of modern IUL products. While the Annuity Illustration Working Group has made incremental updates to annuity illustration models, IUL reforms have remained largely stalled. Regulators did not debate Weber's proposals during the June meeting, citing time constraints, leaving the issue unresolved for now.

The broader regulatory context is significant. AG 49-A was revised in 2023 to tighten illustration limits, with further consumer-protection disclosure enhancements taking effect in 2026. But experts argue these incremental updates are insufficient given the scale of the problem. The NAIC has not yet moved to comprehensively overhaul IUL illustration standards, and the committee's failure to engage with Weber's proposals suggests meaningful reform may still be months or years away.

The stakes are high for the entire life insurance distribution channel. IUL has been one of the fastest-growing life insurance product categories, with Q1 2026 LIMRA data showing IUL premiums up 14% year-over-year to $1.1 billion. As more consumers — particularly middle-income buyers seeking retirement savings vehicles — purchase IUL policies, the potential for widespread consumer harm from misleading illustrations grows proportionally.

★ What This Means for Agents

  • Review how you present IUL: if you sell indexed universal life, this regulatory scrutiny should prompt an immediate audit of your sales conversations and disclosure practices.
  • Don't rely on illustrations alone: presenting only carrier-generated projections without discussing downside scenarios exposes you to significant E&O liability.
  • Stress-test the numbers: supplement standard illustrations with scenarios showing what happens if crediting rates come in 1–2% below illustrated rates.
  • Document risk conversations: record client discussions about variability, expenses, and lapse risk — especially for retirees counting on illustrated income.
  • Get ahead of NAIC reforms: forthcoming disclosure requirements are likely; proactive education now builds trust and protects your practice.

Story 2: Advocates: Life Insurers Potentially Missing Millions of Deaths Annually

Source: InsuranceNewsNet | Published: June 17, 2026

A significant and largely invisible problem in the life insurance industry came into sharp focus at a June 2026 meeting of the NAIC's Life Insurance and Annuities Committee: the federal Death Master File (DMF), the primary tool that many states require insurers to use for identifying deceased policyholders, now captures only 16% of U.S. deaths — down from 95% before 2011. Consumer advocates are warning that this dramatic decline is leaving millions of families without the life insurance benefits their loved ones paid for over decades.

The testimony was delivered by Richard M. Weber, a 59-year veteran of the life insurance industry, who explained the history of the DMF's deterioration. The Social Security Administration's Death Master File was once a comprehensive, reliable database of U.S. deaths. But in 2011, the SSA removed over 4 million records from its public database following concerns about identity theft and fraud. The Bipartisan Budget Act of 2013 imposed further restrictions on access to the DMF, dramatically reducing its coverage. Today, insurers relying solely on the DMF may be unaware of the vast majority of their policyholders' deaths.

"Millions of families risk delayed or lost benefits," Weber stated during the NAIC committee meeting. The practical consequence is stark: when an insurer doesn't know a policyholder has died, it doesn't initiate a claims process. Beneficiaries who don't know a policy exists — or who don't know to file a claim — may never receive the death benefit. The policy simply sits dormant, with premiums potentially continuing to be paid from the estate or the policy lapsing for non-payment.

The scale of the problem is illustrated by the NAIC's own Life Insurance Policy Locator Service (LIPLS), which has uncovered $13 billion in benefits since its 2016 launch. Tennessee alone reported $107.7 million in benefits located for state residents in 2025 — a 22% increase from the prior year. These figures represent only the cases where beneficiaries knew to search; the total universe of unclaimed benefits is almost certainly far larger.

Weber and Kathy Belfi, a former Connecticut insurance regulator, proposed a new NAIC model regulation that would require insurers to search additional death data sources beyond the DMF. Their recommendations include searching state vital records, obituary databases, funeral home records, and commercially available death-data services. They also called for monthly death searches (versus the semiannual or quarterly checks currently common), shortened beneficiary-search timelines of 60 days rather than 90, and annual reporting requirements on death matches and successful beneficiary contacts.

The industry's response was measured but not dismissive. Leah Walters, senior vice president at the American Council of Life Insurers (ACLI), acknowledged the goal of paying beneficiaries but pushed back on the characterization of widespread failure. "We share your goal — we want to pay beneficiaries and keep our promises," she said, citing the success of the LIPLS tool and noting that life insurers paid $223 billion in claims in 2023 and $198 billion in 2024. The ACLI's position is that the industry is already making substantial efforts to locate and pay beneficiaries.

The regulatory landscape is uneven. About half of U.S. states have adopted versions of the National Council of Insurance Legislators (NCOIL) model law on unclaimed life insurance benefits, which requires insurers to conduct periodic death searches and attempt to locate beneficiaries. But the other half have not, creating a patchwork of requirements that varies significantly by state. Advocates argue that a uniform NAIC model regulation would level the playing field and ensure consistent consumer protection nationwide.

The NAIC committee concluded the June meeting without binding action, though Iowa Insurance Commissioner Doug Ommen suggested potential follow-up at future meetings. The lack of immediate action reflects the complexity of the issue — balancing consumer protection with the operational burden on insurers of conducting more frequent and comprehensive death searches — but advocates are pressing for urgency given the scale of potential unclaimed benefits.

For context, the NAIC's LIPLS tool works by allowing beneficiaries, executors, or legal representatives to submit information about a deceased individual. The NAIC then forwards the request to all licensed U.S. life insurance companies, which search their records and respond if a match is found. The tool is free, confidential, and available at eapps.naic.org/life-policy-locator. However, as Weber noted, the tool's effectiveness is limited by the fact that beneficiaries must know to use it — and must know the deceased had a policy in the first place.

The issue has particular relevance for the final expense and senior life insurance market. Many final expense policies are purchased by older adults who may not communicate the details to their families. When those policyholders die, beneficiaries — often adult children — may have no idea a policy exists. The combination of a degraded DMF and beneficiaries who don't know to search creates a perfect storm for unclaimed benefits.

★ What This Means for Agents

  • Give beneficiaries the details: make it standard practice to document policy information and provide written claim instructions — insurer name, policy number, and how to file.
  • Create a policy summary: a simple one-page document clients can leave for family members ensures benefits aren't lost to silence.
  • Promote the NAIC locator: educate clients about the free Life Insurance Policy Locator Service at eapps.naic.org/life-policy-locator for deceased relatives.
  • Know your state's rules: if your state hasn't adopted the NCOIL unclaimed-benefits model law, your clients' families may have fewer automatic protections.
  • Final expense clients are high-risk: older buyers who don't tell their children about a policy are exactly the cases where proactive agent communication matters most.

Story 3: John Hancock Launches Enhanced Protection Variable Universal Life Insurance Solution

Source: PR Newswire / John Hancock | Published: June 17, 2026

John Hancock, one of the largest life insurance companies in the United States and a unit of Manulife Financial Corporation, announced on June 17, 2026, the launch of an enhanced Protection Variable Universal Life (Protection VUL) solution. The product is designed to address the evolving financial needs of Americans facing longer life expectancies, combining traditional death benefit protection with cash value growth potential and optional living benefit riders — a configuration that reflects the industry's broader shift toward "living benefits" as a core product feature.

The announcement comes at a time when the life insurance industry is experiencing strong growth, with LIMRA reporting that Q1 2026 new annualized premiums jumped 10% year-over-year to $4.5 billion. Variable universal life (VUL) premiums specifically increased 12% in Q1 2026 to $729 million, with nearly half of VUL carriers reporting double-digit growth. John Hancock's product launch is clearly timed to capitalize on this momentum and expand its reach in the VUL market segment.

The Protection VUL is a permanent life insurance product that allows policyholders to allocate cash value among a range of investment subaccounts, providing market-linked growth potential alongside a death benefit. The "protection" framing emphasizes the product's death benefit guarantee features, which include a no-lapse guarantee under specific conditions — a critical feature for clients who want the growth potential of a variable product without the risk of losing their death benefit if the market underperforms.

A key differentiator of the enhanced Protection VUL is its integration with the John Hancock Vitality program, which rewards policyholders for healthy behaviors with premium discounts and other incentives. The Vitality program tracks health metrics and activities — such as exercise, preventive screenings, and healthy eating — and translates them into tangible financial benefits for policyholders. This wellness-linked approach is part of John Hancock's broader strategy to position life insurance as a product that enhances life, not just protects against death.

The optional living benefit riders are a particularly significant feature for the senior and pre-retirement market. These riders allow policyholders to access a portion of their death benefit while still alive if they meet certain qualifying conditions — typically related to chronic illness, critical illness, or terminal illness. As Americans live longer and face the dual risks of outliving their savings and incurring significant long-term care costs, living benefit riders have become increasingly important to consumers and agents alike.

Hector Martinez, Head of Insurance at John Hancock, framed the launch in terms of the company's mission to serve Americans facing longer, more complex financial lives. "Living longer is one of the greatest opportunities — and challenges — facing today's families," he said. "Our Protection VUL gives us the opportunity to reach more customers with the flexibility to protect their loved ones with meaningful growth potential as they prepare for the financial realities of a longer life."

The product is available through licensed financial professionals and is not available in all states. As with all variable life insurance products, the cash value is subject to market risk — the investment subaccounts are not FDIC-insured and are not bank-guaranteed. Policyholders who take loans or withdrawals from the policy risk reducing the death benefit, triggering policy lapse, or incurring taxes and penalties.

The launch also signals John Hancock's intent to compete more aggressively in the advisor-distributed market. The company serves over 10 million Americans with life insurance, annuities, and retirement solutions, and the Protection VUL is positioned as a tool for financial professionals to address clients' needs for both protection and accumulation in a single product.

From a market positioning standpoint, the Protection VUL competes in a crowded field. Carriers like Transamerica, Pacific Life, and Lincoln National all offer VUL products with living benefit riders. John Hancock's differentiation lies primarily in the Vitality program integration and the company's brand strength in the health-conscious consumer segment.

The timing of the launch is notable given the regulatory scrutiny of IUL illustrations discussed elsewhere in this newsletter. VUL products, unlike IUL, are securities products regulated by both state insurance departments and the SEC/FINRA. VUL illustrations are subject to different regulatory requirements, including prospectus disclosure requirements that provide more comprehensive risk disclosure than typical IUL illustrations.

★ What This Means for Agents

  • Living benefits are the trend: major carriers like John Hancock are doubling down on wellness-linked products and riders that pay while the insured is still alive.
  • Evaluate for 40–60 clients: Protection VUL targets buyers who want accumulation alongside death benefit protection during longer, more complex retirements.
  • Vitality drives engagement: clients who participate in wellness programs tend to stay more engaged with their policies and lapse less often.
  • Securities licensing required: VUL is a variable product — you need Series 6 or 7 (and state registration) to sell it; consider whether expanding your license fits your practice.
  • Prospectus disclosure is an advantage: compared with IUL under current scrutiny, VUL's SEC/FINRA requirements may offer clearer risk framing for informed buyers.

Story 4: Sixfold Launches AI Underwriter for Insurers with Straight-Through Quote and Bind Capability

Source: Fintech Global | Published: June 17, 2026

Sixfold, an artificial intelligence platform built specifically for the insurance industry, officially launched its AI Underwriter on June 17, 2026 — a product that represents a significant step forward in the automation of insurance underwriting. The launch follows a $30 million Series B funding round completed in January 2026, which was raised specifically to build the product. The AI Underwriter is now available to property and casualty (P&C) insurers, though its underlying technology and implications extend to the broader insurance distribution ecosystem, including life and health carriers exploring similar automation.

The AI Underwriter is built on what Sixfold calls its "Underwriting Brain" — a foundation model pre-trained on professional underwriting credentials, reasoning methodologies, and a curated library of industry-specific knowledge spanning multiple lines and industries. When a submission arrives, the system automatically extracts and cleans all relevant data, identifies gaps in the submission, assesses the risk against the carrier's specific appetite and portfolio guidelines, and produces a recommendation alongside a clear rationale and a suggested next step for the underwriter.

A critical design principle of the AI Underwriter is data sovereignty. Each carrier operates within its own independent instance of the system. The AI learns from that carrier's specific book of business, underwriting decisions, and guidelines — but that data is never used to train models for other clients. This addresses one of the primary concerns insurers have about AI adoption: the risk that proprietary underwriting data and risk appetite information could be shared with or used to benefit competitors.

The performance metrics reported from pilot testing are striking. Sixfold reports that customers have observed processing time reductions ranging from 50% to 97%. Adoption rates among underwriting teams have reached 90% or higher. Some carriers have reported a 15% increase in quote-to-bind ratios and up to 30% growth in gross written premium per underwriter. These are the kinds of productivity gains that can fundamentally change the economics of an underwriting operation.

Skyward Specialty, one of the pilot carriers, provided a detailed endorsement. Melissa Butt, vice president of E&S brokerage property at Skyward Specialty, described the tool's impact: "The AI Underwriter does not just summarize what you are looking at, it gives you a point of view and a clear path forward. For more junior underwriters it's a powerful companion that gives them the perspectives of a more experienced underwriter and accelerates their development."

Sixfold CEO Alex Schmelkin articulated the product's core value proposition: "What does a great underwriter actually need the moment they receive a submission? They don't need a digitized version of the application, broker documents moved from their inbox to another system, or a dashboard with data that was current last week. Instead, our users told us that what they need is to understand the risk in the full context of their current book, the broker, and the guidelines, and to be presented with a clear next step."

The launch is part of a broader wave of AI adoption in insurance underwriting. Deloitte's 2026 Insurance Predictions report found that agentic AI could add $2 billion in annual incremental premiums in the U.S. by 2030 and increase new annualized individual life insurance premiums by 11%. The report also found that 51% of consumers are willing to research and shop for life insurance using AI tools.

For the life insurance and final expense market specifically, the implications of AI underwriting are significant. Accelerated underwriting (AUW) programs have already expanded dramatically, with some carriers now approving face amounts as high as $5 million without traditional medical exams. AI tools like Sixfold's are the engine behind this expansion, enabling carriers to process more applications faster while maintaining underwriting discipline.

The regulatory environment for AI in insurance underwriting is evolving. The NAIC has issued guidance on the use of AI, and many states have adopted the NAIC's AI Model Bulletin, which requires insurers to implement governance frameworks ensuring transparency, fairness, and the prevention of discriminatory outcomes in algorithmic models.

The broader context of insurance industry consolidation is also relevant. The $22 billion merger between Corebridge Financial and Equitable Holdings, announced in March 2026, is expected to drive significant technology investment as the combined entity seeks to achieve over $500 million in annual run-rate expense synergies by 2028. AI-driven underwriting automation is likely to be a key component of those efficiency gains.

★ What This Means for Agents

  • Faster carrier turnaround: insurers using AI underwriting can quote and bind more quickly — agents who submit clean, complete applications will see the biggest benefit.
  • Your role is shifting: as AI handles routine risk analysis, human agents add value through needs assessment, complex health navigation, and trusted guidance.
  • Accelerated underwriting is expanding: AUW programs already approve large face amounts without exams; AI tools are the engine behind that growth in life and FE markets.
  • Watch NAIC AI guidance: states adopting the NAIC AI Model Bulletin require carrier governance on transparency and fairness — know which carriers comply.
  • Consolidation drives tech spend: mega-mergers like Corebridge–Equitable will likely accelerate AI adoption across underwriting operations industry-wide.
