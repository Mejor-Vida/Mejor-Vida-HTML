Your driving record can enter a life insurance application too

September 13, 2026 | Mejor Vida Insurance | About 10 min read

The Wall Street Journal’s Buy Side explained that many applications, including no-exam ones, include a motor-vehicle record. One ticket often does not change the price; several can.

Published: September 13, 2026 · Source: see links at the end of the article

Many families think life insurance looks only at age and health. On September 11, The Wall Street Journal’s Buy Side, in a piece by Kimberly Lankford, explained another part of the file: the motor-vehicle record, or MVR. Life insurers commonly pull it when they review risk, including on many no-exam applications.

An MVR can show moving violations, accidents, impaired-driving convictions, and suspended or revoked licenses. Because each state keeps its own records, companies often use national aggregators to gather that information. You usually do not have to “bring the paper to the appointment.” Signing the application’s authorization typically allows the check.

What usually weighs more — and what does not

One moving violation, such as a speeding ticket, often does not change a quote by itself. Two or more in two or three years can keep an applicant out of the best preferred class: the category for people an insurer considers lower risk. Three or more in three years commonly can mean a standard class or an added charge. Parking tickets and similar non-moving violations typically do not affect life insurance quotes.

Recent DUI, DWI, or reckless-driving convictions get more attention. Depending on the company and how much time has passed, they may lead to postponement, a decline, or a temporary extra charge called a flat-extra: an amount added to the premium for a set number of years. A DUI does not automatically disqualify everyone. It usually makes the best class harder to get. A poor recent record can also block no-exam programs and push the case to a full review.

The article noted that for older applicants, a poor driving record or recent accidents can also be read as a flag for medical, medication, or cognitive issues. That does not mean a crash “proves” an illness. It means the company may ask for more information.

A simple example

Imagine Carlos, 54. He has a speeding ticket from eleven months ago and a DUI from three years ago. He asks for a “preferred” quote he saw in an ad. If the company uses a five-year window for serious convictions, that advertised quote is not his price. Another company, with a different manual, might offer standard or a flat-extra for a few years. The same file does not produce the same result everywhere.

That is why it helps to gather dates before you apply: tickets, accidents, suspensions, and convictions. Accurate information does not “clean” the record. It prevents an expectation the file will not support.

What this could mean for your family

Does this affect you? If you are applying for new coverage, yes: the MVR can enter the file even if you feel healthy. Does it cancel a policy you already have? No. A story about how a new application is reviewed does not change a contract already in force.

Time after a serious offense can sometimes improve the class. Waiting also means you are older. There is no single rule that “after X years you get preferred.” Manuals vary. The NAIC explains that accelerated underwriting uses outside sources — including, in many cases, driving history — to shorten timelines. That does not replace honesty on the application.

What you can do now

Write the history with dates, not a vague memory. Mention suspensions or convictions before you fill out papers. Answer truthfully. If a no-exam program does not fit, ask about a full review instead of insisting on an advertisement.

Frequently asked questions

Will a parking ticket raise my life insurance?

Usually not. The Buy Side article says small non-moving violations typically do not affect life insurance quotes.

Does a DUI mean I will be declined?

Not automatically. It can raise the cost, postpone an offer, or, in some recent or repeat cases, lead to a decline. Rules vary by company.

Does this change a policy I already pay for?

No. It describes how a new application is reviewed. It does not rewrite a contract already in force.
