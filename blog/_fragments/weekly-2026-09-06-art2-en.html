A national cost figure is not your family’s funeral bill

September 6, 2026 | Mejor Vida Insurance | About 10 min read

NFDA figures provide context. A final expense coverage amount should come from local prices and from money your family has already set aside.

Published: September 6, 2026 · Source: see links at the end of the article

Many families pick a final expense amount from a number they heard once: “ten thousand covers a funeral.” Sometimes that number is enough. Sometimes it is not. The National Funeral Directors Association (NFDA) publishes national statistics to give context — not to quote the service in your city.

In its 2023 general price list study, NFDA reported that the national median for a funeral with viewing and burial was $8,300. With viewing and cremation, the median was $6,280. A median is the midpoint of a sorted list of prices. It is not the average, and it is not your bill.

What those figures leave out

NFDA notes that those medians do not necessarily represent the family’s full expense. The burial estimate does not include, for example, the cemetery plot, outer burial container, or monument. Flowers, an obituary, travel, and a gathering after the service can also raise the total.

Costs vary by place, funeral home, and services chosen. A simple funeral in a smaller city does not cost the same as a multi-day visitation in a more expensive market. That is why your local general price list matters more than a national headline when you choose a coverage amount.

Final expense insurance versus a prepaid funeral

Final expense coverage is usually a small whole life policy. It is not an agreement with the funeral home. When the insured person dies and an eligible claim is approved, the company pays the death benefit to the named beneficiary. That person decides how to use the money.

A prepaid funeral is an advance arrangement with a funeral provider for specific goods or services. It serves a different purpose. If you mix the two in your mind — “I already paid for the funeral” when you actually have insurance, or the reverse — the family can face a gap on the day they need clarity.

What this could mean for your family

Suppose the burial median ($8,300) is your starting point. Then the local funeral home plus cemetery add $3,500 for the plot and $800 of extras. The planning total is already near $12,600. If the family has $2,000 saved for this purpose, the coverage conversation starts near $10,600 — not at the $8,300 headline. These figures are an educational example, not a quote and not a price for your city.

The premium also has to be sustainable. A high amount the family cannot pay every month protects no one if the policy lapses. Availability, eligibility, and price depend on age, health, product, and the insurer’s evaluation. This page does not promise a premium or an approval.

Does your current insurance stay the same? Yes, unless you change it or stop paying according to the contract. Should you do something? Ask for the local list and subtract money already set aside. Does it matter if you prefer cremation? It can: the national median for cremation with viewing was lower than burial in that study, but your local total may differ.

What you can do now

Request the general price list. Write cemetery and extras separately. Subtract savings or a prepaid plan that truly covers specific services. Bring that result to a final expense quote. Name a beneficiary and a backup. Answer health questions truthfully.

Frequently asked questions

Are NFDA’s $8,300 my funeral cost?

No. It is a 2023 national median and does not include every cemetery charge or extra. Ask for the local list.

Does final expense insurance pay the funeral home?

Usually not. It pays a cash benefit to the person you name, according to the contract.

Should I buy the same amount as the national median?

Not automatically. Use the median as context and build your number from local prices and money already set aside.
