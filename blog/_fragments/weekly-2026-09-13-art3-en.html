If you died tomorrow, who would struggle to pay the bills?

September 13, 2026 | Mejor Vida Insurance | About 10 min read

A television segment this week returned to a household question: rent and groceries continue even when a paycheck stops. Work coverage does not always follow you.

Published: September 13, 2026 · Source: see links at the end of the article

On September 10, WKMG ClickOrlando published and updated a Dollars & Sense segment built around a kitchen-table question: if you died now, would anyone suffer financially? Reporter Donovan Myrie interviewed CBS News business analyst Jill Schlesinger. She did not start with a product. She started with the bills that do not stop: rent, food, a car loan, children’s costs.

That question is not a new law. It is a way to measure whether the household depends on income — or unpaid work — that would disappear. Schlesinger also stressed that a stay-at-home caregiver creates economic value. Replacing child care and household tasks can cost as much as a paycheck. A family that “has only one income” sometimes has two jobs: one paid and one invisible.

What the station said — and what we will not invent

WKMG said a recent study found that nearly half of Americans would have trouble covering living expenses within six months after a primary wage earner’s death. The page did not name the study. That figure should be read with that limit: it is what the station reported, not a title we can assign to another organization.

Schlesinger described what life insurance can be used for: ongoing living costs, child or elder care, debt, future costs such as school, and a surviving spouse’s later needs. That is not a list of what Mejor Vida Insurance sells in every case. It is a map of gaps a family can write on a sheet of paper.

Term, permanent, and the workplace policy

Term life covers a written period. If the insured dies during that term, and the contract is in force, the company pays the face amount to the named beneficiary: the person chosen to receive the money. The Insurance Information Institute explains that term and permanent forms are different types, not the same contract with another name.

Permanent coverage — whole life or other forms designed to last if the contract is maintained — usually includes a savings piece and costs more at the same face amount. Schlesinger said it is used in fewer cases, such as certain estate planning. That does not mean term is the only useful product, or that permanent coverage never fits. It means the job for the money should come first: how many years, for which bills?

Workplace coverage is convenient. It can also disappear after a job change if it is not portable — if it cannot be kept after you leave. A family that only has that small work policy can be left with no individual life insurance the day the job ends. Asking “does it follow me if I leave?” is as concrete as asking the amount.

What this could mean for your family

Imagine Ana, 41, with two children and a husband who keeps the home. Ana’s job pays the rent. His work avoids $1,200 a month in child care. If Ana were gone, the rent would still be due. If he were gone, that child-care bill would appear overnight. Schlesinger’s question covers both gaps, not only the paycheck.

Does this affect you? If someone would depend on your income or your work at home, yes as a planning question. Should you change a policy today because of a television segment? No. The segment does not modify a contract. Schlesinger observed that term often costs more after age 50; that is a general observation, not a rule that options vanish at 50 or that insurance “is no longer needed.”

What you can do now

Write down who would face hardship and which bills would continue. Check whether workplace coverage is portable. Name a beneficiary and a backup name. Ask for a quote with real information. Do not treat an advertisement as your price.
