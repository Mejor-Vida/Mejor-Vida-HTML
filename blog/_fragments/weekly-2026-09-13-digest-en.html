Applications, driving records, and the bills that keep coming

September 13, 2026 · News from September 6–12, 2026 | Mejor Vida Insurance | About 6 min read

This week: more life insurance applications · driving records · who would pay the bills

Each week we summarize the life insurance news that can help families protect their household. This edition covers three stories from last week: a jump in applications — especially among older adults — how a driving record can enter an application, and a direct question: if you were gone tomorrow, who would struggle to pay the bills?

Story 1: Life insurance applications rose in August, led by older adults

Source: InsuranceNewsNet (MIB Life Index) | Published: September 10, 2026

On September 10, InsuranceNewsNet reported that August 2026 life insurance application activity rose 18% from the same month in 2025. According to the MIB Life Index, that was the strongest year-over-year growth the index has recorded for any August. The index tracks applications among member life insurers. It is not a price list and it is not a count of issued policies. Compared with July, activity was nearly flat, down 0.6%.

Every age group grew year over year, but the largest gains were after age 50: 22.9% for ages 50–59, 34.4% for 60–69, and 53.9% for 70 and older. Through the first eight months of 2026, overall activity was 15.6% higher than the same stretch of 2025. By product, term life applications — coverage for a set period — rose 24.7%. Whole life, permanent coverage that generally builds cash value, rose 23.3%.

Does this affect you? Only as a signal that many people, including older adults, are asking for information. An application is not an approval, and the increase does not mean premiums fell. Health, tobacco, age, amount, carrier, and state still matter. If someone depends on your income or unpaid household work, the practical step is to get real numbers rather than assume it is too late.

Story 2: Your driving record can enter a life insurance application too

Source: The Wall Street Journal (Buy Side) | Published: September 11, 2026

On September 11, The Wall Street Journal’s Buy Side explained that life insurers commonly pull a motor-vehicle record, or MVR, when they review an application. That includes many no-exam applications. An MVR can show moving violations, accidents, impaired-driving convictions, and suspended or revoked licenses.

One speeding ticket often does not change a quote by itself. Two or more moving violations in two or three years can keep someone out of the best preferred class — the category for applicants an insurer considers lower risk. Three or more in three years commonly mean a standard class or an extra charge. Parking tickets usually do not count. A recent DUI does not automatically disqualify everyone, but it often raises the cost or delays an offer. Some companies add a temporary extra charge called a flat-extra.

Should you do anything this week? Write down dates of tickets, accidents, and convictions before you apply. Does this cancel a policy you already have? No. A story about how a new application is reviewed does not change a contract already in force. Carrier rules differ. Complete information helps you avoid expecting a class that the file will not support.

Story 3: If you died tomorrow, who would struggle to pay the bills?

Source: WKMG ClickOrlando | Published: September 10, 2026

On September 10, WKMG ClickOrlando published a Dollars & Sense segment built around a household question: if you died now, would anyone suffer financially? Reporter Donovan Myrie interviewed CBS News business analyst Jill Schlesinger. She noted that rent, food, car payments, and children’s costs continue even when a paycheck stops. Stay-at-home work has value too: replacing child care and household tasks can be expensive.

The station said a recent study found that nearly half of Americans would have trouble covering living expenses within six months after a primary wage earner’s death. The page did not name the study, so that figure should be read with that limit. Term life covers a set period and, if the insured dies during that term, pays the face amount to the named beneficiary. Workplace coverage can be convenient, but families should ask whether it is portable — whether it can be kept after leaving the job.

Does this affect you? If someone would depend on your income or your work at home, yes as a planning question. Should you change a policy today because of a television segment? No. Use the question to review who would collect, which bills would continue, and whether work coverage would follow you.
