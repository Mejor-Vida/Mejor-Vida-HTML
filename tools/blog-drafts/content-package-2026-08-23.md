### Feature — Spanish (~850 words)

Muchas familias creen que “si es información médica, la ley ya la protege.” Esa idea es comprensible. No siempre es correcta cuando el producto es un **seguro de vida**.

Esta semana, publicaciones nacionales —entre ellas *The Wall Street Journal*— volvieron a plantear una pregunta simple: ¿pueden las compañías de seguro de vida usar resultados de pruebas genéticas? Este artículo no copia esa cobertura. Explica lo que **sí** se puede verificar en fuentes oficiales de Estados Unidos, y lo que **no** debemos inventar.

#### Qué está pasando

En 2008, el Congreso aprobó la **Ley de No Discriminación por Información Genética**, conocida como **GINA** (Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act). El Departamento de Salud y Servicios Humanos (HHS) resume el punto que más sorprende a las familias: las protecciones de GINA en materia de **cobertura de salud** **no** se extienden al seguro de vida, al seguro de discapacidad ni al seguro de cuidados a largo plazo.

Dicho en claro: GINA sí limita cómo las aseguradoras de **salud** y muchos **empleadores** pueden usar información genética. **No** convierte en ilegal, a nivel federal, que una aseguradora de **vida** considere información genética.

El Instituto Nacional de Investigación del Genoma Humano (NIH / genome.gov) dice lo mismo: las protecciones de salud de GINA no cubren vida, discapacidad ni cuidados a largo plazo, aunque **algunos estados** tienen leyes propias que añaden límites. Esas leyes varían. No hay una sola regla para todo el país.

Eso es el hecho. No es un pronóstico de que “todas las compañías van a rechazarlo a usted.” Tampoco es un permiso para asustar a nadie. Es un mapa de lo que la ley federal **no** promete.

#### Por qué está pasando ahora

Las pruebas genéticas son más comunes que hace una generación: las pide un médico, a veces un estudio de investigación, a veces un kit en casa. Al mismo tiempo, el seguro de vida sigue haciendo una pregunta antigua: ¿cuál es el riesgo de que esta persona fallezca antes de lo esperado?

Cuando esas dos mundos se cruzan, las familias se preguntan si un resultado en un expediente médico puede aparecer más tarde en una solicitud. GINA se escribió sobre todo para **salud y empleo**, no para igualar todas las líneas de seguro.

#### Quién podría verse afectado

Cualquier persona que:

- esté pensando en un seguro de vida a término, vida entera o gastos finales que **sí** revisa salud, o  
- ya se hizo (o se va a hacer) una prueba genética ordenada por un médico, o  
- vive en un estado con reglas distintas a las de un familiar en otro estado.

Un ejemplo **hipotético**, no un caso real: María, de 48 años, se hace una prueba genética que su médico recomienda. Meses después pide un seguro de vida. Según GINA, esa prueba **no** tiene la misma protección federal que tendría frente a un seguro de **salud**. Lo que ocurra en su solicitud depende de la compañía, del tipo de póliza, de lo que ella autorice revisar y de la ley de su estado. Nadie puede prometerle el resultado por adelantado.

Algunas pólizas de gastos finales hacen **menos** preguntas de salud, o ninguna. Eso no significa que “el ADN no importa nunca.” Significa que **no todos los productos se suscriben igual**. Disponibilidad, elegibilidad y precio varían según la persona, el estado, la compañía y la póliza.

#### Términos en lenguaje sencillo

**Información genética:** no es solo “el kit de ancestralidad.” En la ley federal suele incluir resultados de pruebas genéticas y, en muchos contextos, también antecedentes de enfermedades en la familia.

**GINA:** ley federal de 2008. Título I: seguro de **salud**. Título II: **empleo** (con excepciones, por ejemplo empleadores muy pequeños). HHS señala expresamente que eso **no** cubre vida, discapacidad ni cuidados a largo plazo.

**Suscripción (underwriting):** el proceso en el que una compañía decide si ofrece la póliza y en qué condiciones. No es un diagnóstico médico. Es una decisión comercial sujeta a reglas.

**Seguro de salud vs. seguro de vida:** el primero ayuda a pagar atención médica. El segundo paga un beneficio en efectivo a los beneficiarios si la persona asegurada fallece, según los términos de la póliza. Las leyes que los regulan no son las mismas.

### Feature — English (~850 words)

Many families assume, “If it is medical information, the law already protects it.” That is a fair guess. It is not always true when the product is **life insurance**.

This week, national outlets — including *The Wall Street Journal* — again asked a plain question: can life insurance companies use genetic-testing results? This feature does not reprint that coverage. It explains what U.S. **official sources** actually say, and it stops where the public record stops.

#### What is happening

In 2008 Congress passed the **Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (GINA)**. The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) states the surprise for many households: GINA’s rules against genetic discrimination in **health coverage** **do not** extend to life insurance, disability insurance, or long-term care insurance.

In plain terms: GINA limits how **health** insurers and many **employers** may use genetic information. It does **not**, as a matter of federal law, make it illegal for a **life** insurer to consider genetic information.

The National Human Genome Research Institute says the same thing: GINA’s health-insurance protections do not cover life, disability, or long-term care, though **some states** have their own extra rules. Those rules are not uniform.

That is the verified map. It is not a prediction that “every company will decline you.” It is not a reason to scare anyone away from a doctor-ordered test. It is a reminder of what federal law **does not** promise.

#### Why this is in the news

Genetic tests are more common than a generation ago. Life insurance still asks an old question: what is the chance this person will die sooner than expected? When those two facts meet, families wonder whether a result in a medical chart could show up later on an application. GINA was written mainly for **health insurance and employment**, not to treat every insurance line the same.

#### Who could be affected

Anyone who:

- is shopping for term, whole life, or final expense coverage that **does** review health, or  
- has had (or will have) a clinician-ordered genetic test, or  
- has relatives in another state with different insurance rules.

**Hypothetical example, not a real client:** Maria, 48, takes a genetic test her doctor recommends. Months later she applies for life insurance. Under GINA, that test does **not** have the same federal shield it would have against a **health** insurer. What happens on her application depends on the company, the product, what she authorizes the insurer to review, and her state’s law. Nobody can honestly promise the outcome in advance.

Some final expense policies ask fewer health questions, or none. That does not mean “DNA never matters.” It means **products are not underwritten the same way**. Availability, eligibility, and price vary by person, location, carrier, and policy.

#### Terms in plain language

**Genetic information:** not only an ancestry kit. Under federal law it often includes genetic test results and, in many settings, family medical history.

**GINA:** 2008 federal law. Title I: **health** insurance. Title II: **employment** (with exceptions, such as very small employers). HHS states clearly that this does **not** cover life, disability, or long-term care insurance.

**Underwriting:** the company’s process for deciding whether to offer a policy and on what terms. It is not a medical diagnosis.

**Health insurance vs. life insurance:** one helps pay for care. The other pays a cash benefit to beneficiaries if the insured person dies, according to the contract. Different laws apply.

#### What this could mean for your family

It does not mean you should avoid medical care. It means you should **not assume** a genetic result is invisible to life insurance just because GINA exists.

It also does not mean you should hide facts on an application. Leaving out something the form clearly asks can cause serious trouble later, including at claim time. This article is **not** legal advice about what you must disclose. If a question is unclear, it is reasonable to get licensed help before you sign.

A family whose only goal is funeral costs may need a different product than a family replacing income with term life. Mejor Vida Insurance compares final expense, term, whole life, and other products it is authorized to discuss. None of those options is “approved because an article said so.”

#### What you can do now

1. **Separate health coverage from life coverage** in your mind. GINA is not one shield for every policy.  
2. **Read the application slowly.** If a question about tests or family history is confusing, ask for a plain explanation first.  
3. **Do not guess or hide.** Answer honestly what the form asks.  
4. **Ask how the product is underwritten.** Not every policy asks the same questions.

#### Conclusion

The takeaway: **the federal law that limits genetic discrimination in health insurance does not make the same promise for life insurance.** Understanding that gap is more useful than a frightening headline.

#### Feature — English (~850 words)

Many families hear “annuity” and picture a savings account they can tap when something comes up. That is a fair guess. It is often wrong.

An annuity is an **insurance contract**, not a bank account. The Insurance Information Institute (III) explains that annuities can turn savings into a stream of income, often for retirement. They are not, by themselves, a stand-in for life insurance or a funeral plan.

This week a personal-finance column described a reader who could not use the money until a far-off date. The useful fact is simpler: **if the contract sets a date, a surrender period, or a charge for early withdrawal, that rule governs**, not a headline.

#### What is happening

Some annuities start paying relatively soon (often called immediate). Others wait for years (deferred). Many deferred contracts charge a surrender fee, or allow only a limited free withdrawal, during an initial period. That is not a mysterious bank glitch. It is how the product is built.

III describes annuities as contracts: you give the company money (as a lump sum or over time), and the company promises payments under written rules. Those rules cover when payments start, what it costs to get out early, and what happens at death. Until you read those clauses — or get them in writing — you do not know whether the money is “available” or “locked.”

#### Why this is in the news

Households look for retirement income and, at the same time, worry about a funeral or a short debt. A sales pitch can blur those two jobs. A headline can describe someone frustrated because they “cannot touch their money.” The frustration can be real. The verified lesson is not an anonymous reader’s number. It is this: **access and a payment promise are not the same thing.**

When someone needs cash next week, a contract that waits until maturity does not behave like a final expense policy meant to pay a death benefit. It also does not behave like a checking account.

#### Who could be affected

Anyone who:

- is mixing a CD, a 401(k), and an annuity as if they were the same kind of money, or  
- needs funeral funds and hears “annuity” as if it paid like final expense insurance, or  
- has signed (or is about to sign) without asking when withdrawals are allowed and at what cost.

**Hypothetical example, not a real client:** Luis, 62, puts a work bonus into a deferred annuity. Three years later the family needs funeral money. The contract may allow little or nothing without a penalty until a later date. That is not “theft.” It is a product that was not an emergency account.

Charges and options vary by contract and company. This article does not promise that your annuity will behave like Luis’s, or that you can or cannot withdraw.

**Annuity:** an insurance contract that can pay a stream of money under its terms.

**Immediate vs. deferred:** one may start paying relatively soon; the other usually waits. A marketing name does not replace the contract.

**Surrender charge / maturity period:** the window when access is limited or costly. That is a clause, not an error.

**Life insurance:** pays a death benefit to beneficiaries under the policy, not “cash whenever you want it for something else.”

**Final expense:** a small whole life policy many families use for funeral costs and short debts. It is not an annuity with another label.

#### Feature — Spanish (~850 words)

Cuando alguien fallece, hay que decidir rápido: velorio o no, entierro o cremación, qué ataúd, qué cementerio. La Comisión Federal de Comercio (FTC) escribió una regla precisamente porque esas decisiones se toman con dolor y con poco tiempo.

La **Regla de Funerales** permite elegir solo los bienes y servicios que usted quiere, y pagar solo por eso, ya sea el día del fallecimiento o con anticipación. La FTC aclara que la regla cubre funerarias; no aplica de la misma forma a vendedores de ataúdes por separado ni a cementerios que no tienen funeraria en el lugar.

Esta nota no es un titular de “última hora.” La regla sigue vigente. Aparece en este paquete semanal porque muchas familias no la conocen hasta el día en que más la necesitan — y porque un seguro de gastos finales no sustituye esos derechos.

#### Qué está pasando

La regla no es nueva. Sigue siendo el estándar federal para cómo una funeraria debe mostrar precios y qué puede o no exigirle. Muchas familias nunca la han leído. Por eso vale la misma profundidad que las otras dos notas de la semana: educación práctica, no un alerta falso.

Según las páginas de consumo de la FTC, usted puede:

- pedir **precios por teléfono** sin dar primero su nombre ni su número;  
- recibir, si visita, una **lista general de precios (GPL)** para **quedársela**;  
- ver la lista de precios de ataúdes **antes** de ver los ataúdes;  
- comprar solo lo que necesita, sin aceptar un paquete con extras que no pidió;  
- para cremación, usar un **contenedor alternativo** — ningún estado exige un ataúd tradicional para cremar;  
- traer un ataúd o urna comprados en otro lado; la funeraria no puede negarse ni cobrarle un cargo por “manejarlo”;  
- preguntar por refrigeración u opciones **sin embalsamado**, porque el embalsamado **no** es una obligación legal universal en todos los estados y situaciones.

Esos puntos salen de la FTC. No los inventamos. Si una funeraria local hace algo distinto, esa es una pregunta para esa funeraria y, si hace falta, para la FTC — no una promesa de este artículo.

#### Por qué importa ahora

Las familias cotizan un seguro de **gastos finales** y adivinan “cuánto alcanza.” Sin precios reales de funeral, el número de la póliza es un cálculo a ciegas. La regla existe para que usted pueda comparar en calma: por teléfono, con una lista impresa, sin que le empujen un paquete el día del adiós.

Un seguro no reemplaza estos derechos. El seguro puede poner dinero en la familia. La regla ayuda a que ese dinero no se vaya en cosas que nadie pidió.

#### Quién podría verse afectado

#### Feature — English (~850 words)

When someone dies, decisions come fast: visitation or not, burial or cremation, which casket, which cemetery. The Federal Trade Commission wrote a rule because those choices happen under stress and with little time.

The **Funeral Rule** lets you choose only the goods and services you want and pay only for those, at the time of death or in advance. The FTC notes that the rule covers funeral homes. It does not apply in the same way to third-party casket sellers or to cemeteries with no funeral home on site.

This is not a fake breaking alert. The Rule is still in force. It is in this week’s package because many families never hear of it until the hard day — and because final expense insurance does not replace these rights.

#### What is happening

The Rule is not new. It remains the federal standard for how a funeral home must show prices and what it may or may not require. Many families have never read it. That is why it gets the same depth as the other two notes this week: practical education, not a manufactured scare.

From the FTC’s consumer pages, you may:

- get **prices by phone** without giving your name or number first;  
- receive, in person, a **General Price List (GPL)** you can **keep**;  
- see casket prices **before** you see the caskets;  
- buy only what you need, without a package of extras you did not ask for;  
- use an **alternative container** for cremation — no state requires a traditional casket for cremation;  
- bring a casket or urn purchased elsewhere; the funeral home cannot refuse it or add a “handling” fee;  
- ask about refrigeration or services **without embalming**, because embalming is **not** a universal legal requirement in every state and situation.

Those points come from the FTC. We did not invent them. If a local funeral home does something different, that is a question for that home and, if needed, the FTC — not a promise in this article.

Families shop for **final expense** coverage and guess at “how much is enough.” Without real funeral prices, the policy amount is a blind estimate. The Rule exists so you can compare in calm: by phone, with a printed list, without being pushed into a package on the day of the funeral.

Insurance does not replace these rights. A policy can put money in the family’s hands. The Rule helps that money buy only what the family actually wants.
