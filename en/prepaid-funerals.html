What is a prepaid funeral?

A prepaid funeral (also called a preneed contract) is an agreement with a funeral home: you choose goods and services now and pay some or all of the cost before they are needed. It is not life insurance. This page explains how the contract works, what to ask before you sign, and when cash from a policy may give the family more flexibility.

The problem What it is The contract The money Ask first Questions Next

Three facts to start with

  • You are not buying a generic “funeral.” You are buying the lines named in the contract — casket or urn, transfer, visitation, staff — from the General Price List (GPL): the written menu the funeral home must give you.
  • The FTC says prepaid money is governed by each state. Some states require a percentage to go into a trust (money held under state rules) or a life policy whose payment is assigned to the funeral home. Protections are not the same everywhere.
  • Paying now does not automatically freeze today’s price. Ask in writing whether the price is locked at purchase or set when the death occurs. If it is not guaranteed, the family may owe a balance.

Planning a funeral and prepaying for it are two different decisions. You can write down your wishes without sending money to one funeral home.

The problem families are trying to solve

When someone dies, someone has to pay for care of the body, a resting place, and, if the family wants one, a service. That bill arrives quickly. Medicare does not pay it. Social Security may pay a one-time $255 if its rules are met. That does not cover a typical funeral.

Many people want to spare their children that bill and those decisions. One path is to sign with a funeral home now. Another is to leave cash to a trusted person. They sound alike. They are not the same contract, and the difference shows up if the family moves, changes plans, or the business closes.

What it means in practice

With a prepaid funeral, you and the funeral home agree on goods and services for later. You may pay in full now or pay some now and the rest later, depending on the contract. The FTC Funeral Rule still applies when you plan ahead: you may buy only what you need, and you must receive written prices.

The contract does not turn the funeral home into your bank or into an insurance company. It is a service provider. If the agreement names a casket, a visitation, and a transfer, that is what is promised — not “whatever burial costs” in any city, in any year.

You can also plan without prepaying. The FTC recommends putting preferences in writing, giving copies to the family, and not leaving the only copy in a will or a safe-deposit box that nobody can open on the weekend of the funeral.

What the contract often includes

Funeral-home lines you choose: transfer, care of the body, use of the facilities, service staff, a casket or urn, and the basic fee almost every funeral home publishes.

What is often extra

A plot or niche, opening and closing the grave, a vault if the cemetery requires one, a marker, flowers, an obituary, and travel for relatives. Confirm each line. Do not assume “prepaid funeral” includes the cemetery.

What the law lets you choose

You can refuse a full package. You can bring a casket or urn bought somewhere else. For cremation, you may use a simple alternative container; no state requires a display casket to cremate.

How the contract is put together

Ask for the GPL before you choose. Compare at least two funeral homes. Mark only the lines you want. Then the funeral home must give you a written statement of each good or service and the total, before you pay.

If you pay over time, read what happens if you stop paying, whether extra charges apply, and whether the line prices stay fixed. There is no national interest rate we can cite here: that figure, if it exists, is in the contract itself.

After the death, the family can still change something. If they change the plan or must pay more because prices were not guaranteed, the Funeral Rule again requires price lists and a written statement.

Where the money you pay in advance goes

The FTC is clear: how prepaid funds are handled is set by each state, and some state laws offer little effective protection. Before you hand over money, ask which of these paths that contract uses.

Path 1

A state-regulated trust

Some states require a percentage of the payment to go into a regulated trust. Ask what percentage is deposited, who the trustee is, and what happens to the interest.

Path 2

Insurance assigned to the funeral home

Some contracts are funded with a life policy whose payment is assigned to the funeral home: that business is named to collect the benefit and provide the service. Ask who owns the policy and whether you can change providers.

What you should ask

Which path does this contract use?

We do not guess a funeral home’s trust percentage or policy. If they will not put it in writing, do not sign.

If the funeral home closes, is sold, or changes owners, protection depends on those state rules and the contract text — not on one federal guarantee.

What is usually covered — and what is not

A prepay is not a magic “whole funeral” bundle. It is the list you signed. Two contracts in the same city can include different things under the same sales name.

Usually included, if it is written down

  • Funeral-home services you marked on the GPL.
  • Funeral-home merchandise you chose, such as a specific casket or urn.
  • Sometimes a death certificate or other paperwork, only if the contract names it.

Often not included unless it says so

  • A plot or niche, opening the grave, and cemetery rules.
  • A headstone, flowers, food, airfare, and clergy fees.
  • A funeral in another city if you moved and the contract cannot be transferred.

The Funeral Rule generally does not cover cemeteries that have no funeral home on site, or outside casket and monument sellers. Those bills can still arrive.

When it can help — and where it falls short

It may make sense if

  • You want the service lines chosen with a funeral home you trust, and you do not plan to move.
  • The contract locks those line prices in writing, and you can pay them now without leaving the family short of cash.
  • The family knows where the contract is, so they do not pay for the same funeral twice.

It falls short if

  • The family may live in another city when the time comes. The FTC says some plans can be transferred — often at added cost.
  • Prices are not guaranteed. Then the prepay does not “protect against inflation”: the family may still owe more.
  • You want someone to receive cash for debts, travel, or a simpler cremation. Value is usually tied to the contract lines, not to an unrestricted check.

We do not publish that a prepaid funeral “always” covers rising prices. The FTC describes the other case: survivors may be asked to pay extra when the plan did not freeze the price.

Six questions before you hand over money

These questions come from the FTC’s own list for people thinking about prepaying. Get the answers in the contract, not only in conversation.

  1. What are you paying for? Only merchandise (casket, urn, vault), or funeral services as well?
  2. What happens to the money you prepay? Does it go into a trust, a policy assigned to the funeral home, or another arrangement?
  3. If the money is in a trust, who keeps the interest?
  4. Are you protected if that firm goes out of business?
  5. Can you cancel and get a refund if you change your mind? Is the contract irrevocable (it cannot be undone) or can it be canceled?
  6. What happens if you move or die away from home? Can the plan be transferred, and at what cost?

Tell the family the contract exists and where it is. If nobody knows, they may pay for the same service again.

What it costs to prepay

The price of a prepay is the price of the lines you choose, not a national product with one sticker. We do not publish a “prepaid plans cost X to Y” range: that is not an official median for a contract, and mixing it with an average funeral would mislead the reader.

To size the bill you might be paying in advance, use the same public funeral-home figures we teach on how much a funeral costs. The NFDA published 2023 medians of $8,300 for a funeral with viewing and burial, and $6,280 with viewing and cremation. A plot, vault, and marker are usually extra.

Frequently asked questions

Is a prepaid funeral the same as final expense insurance?

No. A prepaid funeral is a contract with a funeral home for goods or services. Final expense insurance is a life policy: it pays cash to the person you named. That person decides where to spend it.

Does prepaying lock in today’s price?

Only if the contract says so in writing. Some plans freeze the price at purchase; others set it when the death occurs. If it is not guaranteed, the family may owe a balance.

Can I pay in installments?

Many contracts let you pay some or all of the cost in advance. If there is a payment plan, extra charges and what happens if you stop paying are in that contract. There is no national rate we can cite.

What if I move?

Some plans can be transferred, often at added cost. Others stay tied to that funeral home. Ask before you sign. Cash from a life policy travels with the person you named.

What if the funeral home closes?

It depends on your state’s law and whether the money is in a trust or in assigned insurance. The FTC warns that some state laws offer little effective protection. Get that answer in writing.

Can I cancel and get my money back?

It depends on the contract and the state. An irrevocable arrangement cannot be undone at will. A cancelable one may return some or all of the money, sometimes minus fees. Read it before you pay.

Does it include the cemetery plot?

Not automatically. The plot, opening and closing, and sometimes a vault are often separate bills. The Funeral Rule does not cover a cemetery without an on-site funeral home in the same way.

Does the Funeral Rule still apply if I pay now?

Yes. You may buy items separately, ask for the GPL, and bring a casket or urn from another seller. If the family changes the plan or must pay more, they must also receive written prices.

How much should I prepay?

The amount is the lines you choose on the GPL, plus cemetery items if you buy those separately. A national average is not your bill. Start with the funeral cost guide and ask for two local GPLs.

Does SSI count a prepaid funeral as a resource?

Social Security may exclude up to $1,500 per person in burial funds if they are kept separate and clearly set aside for burial. A funeral contract can fall under those rules. The exact figure, and whether the contract is irrevocable, changes the count. Confirm with Social Security; this is not benefits advice.

Do Medicare or Medicaid pay for a prepaid funeral?

Medicare is health insurance: we do not treat it as a funeral plan. Medicaid is run by each state; we do not publish a nationwide “Medicaid pays the prepay” rule. If you receive SSI or Medicaid, talk with that office before signing an irrevocable contract.

Should I tell my family?

Yes. The FTC warns that if nobody knows you already paid, they may pay for the same funeral again. Leave the contract where the family can find it — not only in a safe-deposit box.

If you want to leave cash, not a package

Final expense insurance is a small whole life policy: coverage meant to last a lifetime if the premiums — the regular payments — stay current. The beneficiary is the person you name. The death benefit is the cash that person receives. It does not reserve a service at a specific funeral home.
