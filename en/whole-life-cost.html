What whole life is — and how it works

Whole life is permanent coverage: if premiums stay current, the policy does not end just because you reach a certain age (unlike many term plans).

Three pieces usually come together:

  1. Level premium. On most level plans (including many final-expense designs), the premium does not rise after issue.
  2. Guaranteed death benefit. The contracted amount pays to the beneficiary per the contract.
  3. Cash value (on many plans). Part of the premium may build value with contractual guarantees; loans or withdrawals reduce the benefit if not repaid — Julie shows the impact before you sign.

That’s why it usually costs more per dollar than term: you pay for lifelong coverage and those guarantees, not only a limited risk period.

What raises (or lowers) the price

Start with these variables. Race, ethnicity, sexual orientation, marital status, credit score, and education level are not typically used to set premiums.

Age

Older at issue means a higher price. After issue, whole life premiums usually stay level.

Gender

Women usually pay less. In Montana, unisex rates can price women like men.

Tobacco / nicotine

Cigarettes raise the price. Other forms depend on the carrier. These tables are non-tobacco.

Health

History and medications can raise the price, limit the face amount, or change the plan type.

Amount

More benefit = higher premium (almost always proportional per $1,000).

State

Drives product availability. Julie serves Nebraska, Kansas, Colorado, and Nevada.

Age at issue (not afterward)

Age on the application drives price. In our engine, age 45 is about $23/$27 per month for $10,000; age 70 is about $53/$75 (female/male). After issue, most level whole life premiums do not rise just because you get older. Many carriers price each exact age; a few use age bands.

Gender

In most states, men pay more (often about 30%) because they live fewer years on average. Montana requires unisex rates on some products, which can change that gap.

Tobacco and nicotine

Cigarettes usually place you in a smoker class. Pipe, chew, cigars, or e-cigarettes are not always treated the same — each carrier has its own rule. This page’s tables assume non-tobacco.

Health

Better health usually opens the Level/Immediate band (the one in our tables) and higher face amounts. More serious conditions can mean graded plans, lower amounts, or declines. Honest answers protect a future claim.

Coverage amount

Premium is usually set per $1,000, then multiplied. In our engine, $25,000 costs about 2.5× a $10,000 policy for the same profile — use the tabs to see it.

Riders (optional extras)

Accelerated benefits, accidental death, or child coverage may be included or add cost. Julie only recommends riders that clearly help your plan.

Underwriting type

Three common paths (detail below): simplified issue with no exam (powers these tables), guaranteed acceptance (almost always with a wait), and fully underwritten with an exam (higher faces, often better cost per dollar).

Lifestyle and alcohol

High-risk hobbies, DUI history, or heavy alcohol use can affect rating. Hiding information on the application can void a claim.

State of residence

Forms, products, and sometimes pricing rules depend on where you live and sign. Julie is licensed in Nebraska, Kansas, Colorado, and Nevada.

Lifetime-pay vs. limited-pay

Lifetime pay: you pay until the policy ends (death or cancelation). Usually the lowest monthly premium.

Limited-pay: you pay more each month for a set window; then the policy is “paid up” and you owe no more premiums (per the contract).

  • 7-Pay / 10-Pay / 20-Pay — premiums for 7, 10, or 20 years.
  • Paid up at 65 or 80 — payments stop at that age (useful near retirement).

Limited-pay is rarely “cheaper” overall: it concentrates cost into fewer years. Julie can illustrate both options with the same carrier when it fits (for example Transamerica Lifetime products in her book).

Term or whole life — which fits?

Use this quick comparison:

  • Term — set period (10, 20, 30 years, or to a certain age). Cheaper per dollar. When it ends, you typically get nothing back and may be uninsured.
  • Whole life — permanent need (family, lifelong debts, legacy, funeral). Level premiums and coverage that does not expire by age if kept in force.

Many families combine both: term for the mortgage or child-raising years, and whole life (or final expense) for permanent needs. See term cost charts.

Three ways to qualify

1. Simplified issue (no exam)

Health questionnaire; carriers often check prescriptions (Rx) and motor vehicle records (MVR). No blood draw or paramed visit. In Julie’s book, typical final-expense / simplified whole life faces run about $2,000–$50,000 (Living Promise up to $50,000 on level; American Amicable Senior Choice / Golden Solution by product). This path powers the tables on this page.

2. Guaranteed acceptance

No health questions or exam; you won’t be declined for medical history. Almost always a two-year waiting period: death in that window usually returns premiums paid plus interest (often ~10%), not the full death benefit. Usually the most expensive path. See illustrative GI rates on the hub.

3. Fully underwritten (with exam)

Typically includes health questions, urine and/or blood, blood pressure, and height/weight; sometimes medical records.

  • Pros: often better cost per dollar and higher face amounts (on some products a practical minimum near $100,000).
  • Cons: longer process; higher minimums; not everyone wants an exam.

Fully underwritten permanent products in Julie’s book (for example Transamerica Lifetime) are illustrated case by case. We do not publish $250,000–$2,000,000 charts without real engine rates.

How to get closer to a better price

  1. Answer the questionnaire honestly — opens Level/Immediate classes and protects the claim.
  2. Compare multiple carriers — none is cheapest for everyone; Julie shops for you.
  3. Choose limited-pay only if the monthly budget fits — it is not a total-cost shortcut.
  4. Don’t overbuy — match face amount to debts, income replacement, and legacy; for funeral-only needs, $5,000–$20,000 often suffices based on local costs.
  5. Work with an independent agent licensed in your state.

Frequently asked questions

What’s a typical monthly cost?

It depends on age and face amount. With Mejor Vida’s engine (MOO + American Amicable, Level/Immediate): at age 50 about $24/mo (female) or $31/mo (male) for $10,000; at age 65 about $41/$56 for $10,000; at age 45 about $57/$68 for $25,000. Quote for your real profile.

What determines the cost?

Mainly: issue age, sex, tobacco/nicotine, health, face amount, riders, underwriting type, lifestyle, and state. Race, credit, and education are not typically used to set premiums.

Does it get more expensive just because I get older after buying?

Age at issue raises the price (see the table from 45 to 85). After issue, most level whole life premiums do not increase with the years if you keep the policy in force.

Do I need a medical exam?

Not always. Simplified issue (the basis of these tables) and guaranteed acceptance skip it. Fully underwritten usually includes one. An exam often unlocks lower premiums per dollar and higher face amounts.

Whole life or universal life?

Whole life emphasizes level premiums and, on many plans, cash-value guarantees. Universal / IUL is more flexible and more complex: actual cost depends on funding and charges. Julie can illustrate UL if it fits. UL chart on the hub.

Is there whole life for a child?

Yes: some carriers offer juvenile whole life with limited face amounts, to lock in early insurability and build cash value. Julie reviews options by state and carrier.

Why does state matter?

Products, forms, and pricing rules depend on where you live and sign. Julie is licensed in Nebraska, Kansas, Colorado, and Nevada.

How do I get coverage without a long waiting period?

Look for simplified issue or fully underwritten level-benefit plans (no wait or a short wait) — that is the Level/Immediate band in our tables. Guaranteed acceptance almost always has a two-year wait.
