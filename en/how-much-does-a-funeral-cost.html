How much does a funeral cost?

A funeral bill is usually more than one charge. It can include the funeral home, the cemetery, and extras the family chooses, such as flowers or a headstone. This page explains those costs, shows national figures as a starting point, and shows how to ask a local funeral home for its written price list.

The bill What’s included How it’s quoted Figures Questions How to pay

Three facts to start with

  • There are four common paths: a funeral with service and burial, burial with no viewing, a funeral with service and cremation, and cremation with no service. Price follows that choice, not a single “U.S. average.”
  • In 2023 the NFDA published a national median of $8,300 for a funeral with viewing and burial, and $6,280 with viewing and cremation. Plot, vault, marker, and flowers are usually extra.
  • Ask for the general price list (GPL): the written menu the funeral home must give you before you choose. The FTC Funeral Rule lets you buy only what you need.

A national average is not your city’s invoice. Use it to see the size of the bill, then ask for a local GPL or use the state calculator.

The bill that lands on the family

When someone dies, someone pays for care of the body, a resting place, and a service if the family wants one. Medicare does not pay that bill. Social Security may pay $255 once if its rules are met. That does not cover a funeral.

Families often mix two questions: “what does the funeral home charge?” and “what does insurance cost to leave that money?” They are not the same. This page teaches the first. The second is on the insurance price guide and final expense insurance.

What is inside a funeral bill

The FTC describes three basic arrangements people treat as one product. A traditional funeral (sometimes called full-service) often includes transfer, care of the body, a viewing, a service, then burial or cremation. A direct burial takes the body to the cemetery with no viewing or funeral-home service. A direct cremation cremates the body without that viewing; the family may use a simple container instead of a display casket.

Those labels describe the type of service, not a fixed price. Two funeral homes in the same city can bundle different lines under the same name. The next step is not memorizing an average. It is reading which lines are on the GPL.

What the funeral home usually bills

Transfer, care of the body, use of the facilities, staff for the service, a casket or urn, and the non-declinable basic services fee almost every funeral home publishes.

What the cemetery usually bills

Plot or niche, opening and closing, sometimes an outer container if the cemetery requires one, and the marker. Those lines are often missing from a “funeral with viewing” median.

What the family adds

Flowers, an obituary, travel, a meal, clergy, or a reception. Those are optional and vary widely.

How prices are quoted, in practice

Before you choose, ask for the general price list (GPL): the written menu of goods and services. The FTC Funeral Rule says you should be able to see it. You may also buy a casket or urn elsewhere; the funeral home cannot refuse to use it or add a fee just for that.

You do not have to buy a whole “package.” You can take the basic fee, the transfer, and a direct cremation, and skip the viewing. You can want the viewing and a simple casket. The right price is the lines you will actually use.

If someone gives you a spoken total, ask that it match the GPL. An internet average does not replace that sheet.

National figures, read carefully

There are two public sets, from different years, that measure different things. We do not mash them into one “2026 price.” Read them as a size check, not as a quote from a funeral home.

NFDA 2023 medians (funeral with viewing)

A median is the middle value: half of the cases were lower, half were higher. For 2023 the NFDA published $8,300 for a funeral with viewing and burial, and $6,280 for a funeral with viewing and cremation. Those figures are the funeral-home package. They are not trying to guess the plot or the marker.

What to take from this: a funeral with a visit and a service is a multi-thousand-dollar bill, and cremation with the same kind of service is not “free” — it is still a large bill, only lower than burial with a viewing.

Some trade write-ups of that same study mention a higher total when a vault is added. We could not reopen NFDA’s original press page when this page was written, so we do not publish that extra figure. Confirm a vault on the GPL and with the cemetery.

State package averages (Funeralocity, July 2026)

An average adds the values and divides. Funeralocity publishes a package average by state for four arrangements. The chart below is the average of those 50 states plus D.C., all as of July 2026. It includes paths with no viewing, which the NFDA “with viewing” median does not show.

Funeral with service, burial$8,532
Burial with no viewing$5,109
Funeral with service, cremation$6,246
Cremation with no service$2,217

What to take from this: skipping the viewing lowers the bill in a clear way. Direct cremation is, on average, the lowest of these four paths. A funeral with service and burial is the highest. Your city can sit outside that order.

Across those states, the full-service burial package averaged from about $7,554 (Oregon) to about $9,697 (Minnesota). Direct cremation averaged from about $1,332 (Oregon) to about $3,183 (North Dakota). That is spread, not a quote.

What is usually extra

Even after you pay the funeral-home package, the cemetery may bill a plot, opening and closing, an outer container if required, and the marker. Flowers, food, and family travel are usually not in those medians either. Add those lines and the total can sit well above $8,300 or $6,280.

Cemetery

Plot or niche, open and close, and sometimes rules about a vault or marker style.

Casket or urn

A display model can cost far more than a simple container. You may buy it elsewhere.

After the service

Obituary, flowers, a reception, clergy, and a plane ticket for a child who lives far away.

Why your city will not match the average

Cost follows the local market: how many funeral homes compete, whether there is an on-site crematory, land prices, and what the family chooses. An expensive state average does not mean every town in that state is expensive. Ask for two or three GPLs. Use the funeral cost calculator for a state-level estimate, not as an invoice.

What these figures do not forecast

Funeral prices move with the rest of the economy. The Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes a funeral-expenses index, but one year’s change is not a promise for the next 30. We do not draw a line to 2056: the future is not on today’s GPL.

NFDA projected that in 2025 cremation would be 63.4% of dispositions and burial 31.6%. That describes what families choose, not an automatic discount. “Green” funerals interest many people; we do not publish a national price because there is no verified median that covers them.

Frequently asked questions

What is usually the lowest-cost path?

In Funeralocity’s July 2026 averages, cremation with no service is the lowest of the four packages. That does not require anyone to choose it, and a given funeral home may charge more or less.

Does the $8,300 include the cemetery plot?

Usually no. The 2023 NFDA median is a funeral with viewing and burial on the funeral-home side. Plot, opening, vault, and marker are usually other bills.

Can I buy the casket somewhere else?

Yes. The FTC Funeral Rule lets you bring a casket or urn bought elsewhere. The funeral home cannot refuse to use it or add a fee just for that.

Why do two funeral homes in the same city charge different amounts?

They bundle different lines, have different costs, and compete differently. Compare GPLs, not one spoken total.

Will funeral prices double in 30 years?

We do not know, and we do not publish that forecast. Use today’s GPL and, if you want a cushion, leave cash or a separate contract. Past inflation does not lock in the future.

Does Social Security pay for the funeral?

It may pay $255 once if its rules are met. That does not cover a typical funeral.

Does Medicare pay for the funeral?

Medicare is medical insurance. We do not treat it here as a funeral plan. More detail in the guide on how Medicare relates to final expenses.

Does the VA help with a Veteran’s burial?

Sometimes, if Department of Veterans Affairs rules are met. For a non-service-connected death on or after October 1, 2025, VA publishes a $1,002 burial allowance and $1,002 for a plot. For a service-connected death (on or after September 11, 2001), the published burial maximum is $2,000. Eligibility is not automatic. Confirm on the VA page.

Can I deduct funeral costs on my income tax return?

Generally not as a personal expense on a survivor’s Form 1040. Some estate-tax rules are different. IRS Publication 559 covers survivors. This is not tax advice.

Is an autopsy required in order to have a funeral?

The funeral itself does not require an autopsy. A medical examiner or coroner may order one under state rules and the circumstances of the death. That is not a funeral-home “package extra.”

Is final expense insurance the same as prepaying at the funeral home?

No. A prepaid funeral is a contract with a funeral home for goods or services. Final expense insurance is a life policy: it pays cash to the person you named. Compare both in the prepaid funeral guide.

How much insurance should I buy?

Start with a local GPL, add cemetery and a cushion if you want travel or small debts, then look at the monthly premium you can keep. A national average is not your amount. The calculator helps size the bill; a quote confirms the premium.

How families pay that bill

Families use savings, a joint account, a collection, a contract already paid at a funeral home, Veteran benefits if they apply, or cash from a life policy. None of those paths is “the right one” for everyone. The full guide is how to pay for a funeral.

Savings or an account

If the money is already set aside and the family can use it at the time, you may not need a new product of the same size.

A funeral-home contract

A prepay can lock goods or services with one provider. If you move, that contract can be hard to move. It is not life insurance.

Cash from a policy

The beneficiary receives money and decides how to spend it. The funeral home is paid only if that person pays them.

If you want to leave cash, not a package

Final expense insurance is a small whole life policy: coverage that does not end after 10 or 20 years if you pay on time. The premium is the regular payment. The beneficiary is the person you name. The death benefit is the cash that person receives. It does not reserve a service at a specific funeral home.

That product is taught in full on final expense insurance. Monthly price — age, health, tobacco, amount — is on the insurance cost guide. If the goal is only a cremation, see also cremation insurance: at the companies we quote it is the same kind of policy, not a crematory package.
