How to plan your funeral

Write down what you want for your funeral so your family is not guessing under pressure. Include burial or cremation, whether you want a visitation, and who they should call. You do not have to pay a funeral home in advance to leave those instructions.

The rush What planning is Decisions Compare Questions Next

Three facts to start with

  • You may decide on arrangements without paying in advance. Prices may go up; a business may close; in some places prices may also go down. Review the written notes every few years, and make sure the family knows where they are.
  • Put preferences in writing, give copies to family and to your attorney, and keep one copy handy. Do not leave them only in a will —it is often read after the funeral— or as the only copy in a safe-deposit box, which may not open on a weekend or holiday.
  • In 2023, NFDA published a median of $8,300 for a funeral with viewing and burial, and $6,280 with viewing and cremation, on the funeral-home side. Plot, vault, and marker are often extra. That is the size of the bill, not a price for “planning.”

Planning and prepaying are two different jobs. You can leave clear instructions and, later, decide whether the money comes from savings, a prepaid contract, a life policy, or a mix. See also how a funeral is paid.

Why families feel rushed

When someone dies, there are usually only a few days to choose a funeral home, a type of service, and, if there is a burial, a cemetery space. The people who sign are tired and often have not compared prices. A calm written note does not remove grief; it does reduce the choices that have to be invented on the spot.

Planning is not the same as “settling everything with one funeral home today.” It is a record of what matters —and what you do not want— so the person who signs knows where to start.

What planning means (and what it is not)

A funeral plan, on this page, is a record of preferences: what happens to the body, whether there is a visitation or a service, whom to call, and where the papers are. By itself it is not a contract and not a payment.

A prepaid funeral is a contract with a funeral home (or, in some states, another licensed seller) for specific goods or services, paid in part or in full before they are needed. That contract has its own rules about the money, moving, and canceling. The dedicated guide is prepaid funerals.

The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) explains that you may decide on arrangements without sending money in advance. If you later prepay, ask where the money is held and what happens if you move or the firm closes —and tell the family, so they do not pay twice.

What you want

Type of service, whether there is a visitation, music or a rite, and whether you prefer burial, cremation, or body donation if that is your wish.

Who has it

Copies with family, with your attorney if you have one, and a copy that can be read the same day, not only in a distant file.

How it will be paid

That can be decided later. Savings, a contract already paid, or cash from a life policy are different paths. They do not have to live on the same paper as the preferences.

How to write it so it actually gets used

Write it in language your family will understand. Name a funeral home or cemetery only if you have already chosen; if not, say “compare at least two funeral homes” and note the type of service. The FTC recommends giving copies to family and to an attorney, and keeping one copy handy.

Do not put preferences only in a will. A will is often read after the funeral. Do not leave the only copy in a bank safe-deposit box: a weekend or holiday can delay access when it is needed most.

Review the writing every few years. Prices change, a funeral home may close or be sold, and your own ideas may change. If you prepaid, review that contract too; it is not the same as this preference note.

Three places that are not enough on their own

Only the will

It is often opened after the funeral. It handles property; it should not be the only place for the visitation or the cremation.

Only the safe-deposit box

Access may wait for bank hours. Leave another copy with someone who can read it the same day.

Only “I told someone”

That person may forget details or be unavailable. A short, dated paper is easier to follow.

Which kind of service you are choosing

The FTC describes three common paths. Culture, faith, budget, and what you want for the people who remain matter more than a national average. No type is required.

Traditional (full-service) funeral

Usually includes a visitation or viewing, a service, and then burial or cremation. It is generally the most expensive path on the funeral-home side, because there are more hours, staff, and facilities.

Direct burial

The body is buried without a viewing and, typically, without embalming. A funeral home or cemetery will still charge for transfer, a casket or container, and opening and closing the grave.

Direct cremation

Cremation without a prior viewing. Under the FTC Funeral Rule, the funeral home must offer an alternative container (it does not have to be an expensive casket) if you do not want one. A memorial service can be held later, with or without the ashes present.

Some people write that they want to donate the body to a medical school or hospital. Each program sets its own rules; we do not invent which ones accept. Note it as a preference and leave the contact the family should call. A “green” burial (no vault, or fewer chemicals) is also a preference: ask the cemetery whether it allows that. There is no national price we can cite.

Six decisions that fit on one page

You do not need a ten-chapter manual. These six questions cover what the family usually has to settle first. You may write “my daughter decides” on a line; that is still an instruction.

  1. What happens to the body. Burial, cremation, or donation if that is your wish. If you choose burial, say whether a plot already exists or one must be bought.
  2. Whether there is a visitation or a rite. Viewing, a church service, a chapel service, or none. Note faith, language, and whether you want flowers or donations.
  3. Which funeral homes to compare. Name one or two, or ask the family to request the general price list (GPL: the price document a funeral home must show) at more than one place.
  4. The cemetery, if there is a burial. Plot, marker or vault rules, and whether a VA national cemetery should be checked.
  5. Who pays, and from where. Savings, a contract already signed, or cash from a life policy. The detail is in how to pay for a funeral. Here it is enough to name the person who has the papers.
  6. Who has copies, and when you review. Names, phone numbers, and a date to reread the note (for example, every two or three years).

How to compare funeral homes with the general price list (GPL)

The general price list (GPL) is the document a funeral home must show you if you ask in person about goods or services. By phone, they must give you the prices you ask for. The law does not require them to mail you the GPL. Compare more than one provider: a neighborhood name does not guarantee an independent owner; ask whether the business is corporately owned if that matters to you.

They may sell packages, but they must also give you the itemized list so you do not buy lines you do not need. The Funeral Rule covers funeral homes; it generally does not cover cemeteries or mausoleums unless that business also sells funeral goods and services.

In most states, the FTC says you are not legally required to use a funeral home to plan or carry out a funeral. That does not mean there is no paperwork: the death certificate and permits still follow state rules. We do not publish a state-by-state list of who may sign those papers, because it varies and goes stale. Many families still hire a funeral home for transfer and timing.

The cemetery plot, before it is urgent

Buying a cemetery space in a rush after a death is common. The FTC notes that it is usually in the family’s interest to buy the plot before need, so you can choose location, the faith of the grounds, and rules on monuments, flowers, or a vault. Those costs —opening and closing the grave, perpetual care— are often separate from the funeral-home bill. The breakdown is in how much a funeral costs.

If there is military service, a Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) national cemetery may be an option for Veterans with a discharge other than dishonorable, and in some cases for spouses and children, under published rules. It is not automatic for every Veteran. Confirm eligibility on VA.gov. Some funeral homes advertise “veteran specials”; ask for the full price in writing. There is a separate VA burial allowance, with amounts by date of death and whether the death was service-connected; that is not the same as a grave in a national cemetery.

Some older consumer articles say “all veterans” get a free burial in a national cemetery. The current VA page is narrower (type of discharge, serious crimes, and other exceptions). We use VA.gov, not that sweeping phrase.

Why money comes later on this page

Planning does not have a “national price” we can cite. What is published is the usual size of the funeral-home bill, so you know the order of magnitude the family is talking about when it is time to pay.

Funeral with viewing and burial — funeral-home side. Plot and marker are often extra.

NFDA 2023 median

$6,280

Funeral with viewing and cremation — funeral-home side. Urn, niche, or scattering may be extra.

These are 2023 national medians, not a quote for your city. A simpler GPL service usually costs less; a private cemetery can add thousands. Use the cost guide and the calculator for order of magnitude, not as an invoice.

Social Security may pay $255 once if a spouse or children meet its rules. That does not cover a funeral with visitation. Medicare is medical insurance; it does not pay for the funeral. Payment paths are in how to pay for a funeral.

What a written plan helps — and what it does not fix

What it usually helps

  • The family knows the type of service and whom to call, without inventing it under pressure.
  • You can compare the GPL calmly and not feel required to take a full package.
  • If there is a plot or a VA cemetery to check, that is noted before the rush.

What it does not guarantee

  • It does not freeze future prices, unless you sign a prepaid contract with clear rules.
  • It does not bind a funeral home that never signed it. It is a guide for the person who contracts.
  • It does not replace cash. If there are no savings, no prepaid contract, and no policy, the family still has to gather payment.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to pay now in order to plan?

No. You can write preferences without sending money to a funeral home. Paying in advance is a separate contract, with state rules about the funds. If you will not prepay, still leave the writing and the copies.

Is it enough to put this only in my will?

A will matters for property, but it is often read after the funeral. Put preferences in a separate note and give copies to the person who will sign with the funeral home.

Can I leave the only paper in a safe-deposit box?

Not as the only copy. The bank may be closed. Leave another copy with someone you trust and, if you have an attorney, one with that person.

Do I have to use a funeral home?

In most states, the FTC says it is not a legal requirement to plan or hold the funeral. Paperwork (death certificate, permits) still follows state rules. We do not list every state here. Many families still hire a funeral home for transfer and timing.

What is the difference between a traditional funeral, direct burial, and direct cremation?

Traditional usually includes a visitation and a service, and is generally the most expensive. Direct burial skips the viewing. Direct cremation cremates without a prior visitation; they must offer you an alternative container. A memorial can be held later.

When should we buy a cemetery plot?

The FTC says it is usually in the family’s interest to buy before need, so you can choose location and rules without rushing. Ask about vault, marker, flowers, and opening and closing fees. A cemetery is not covered by the Funeral Rule the same way a funeral home is, except in limited cases.

Can I donate the body?

You can write that as a preference. Each school or hospital sets its own rules; we do not claim which ones accept or what costs remain for the family. Leave a contact number.

What is a “green” burial?

It usually means fewer chemicals or no concrete vault, depending on the cemetery. There is no national price standard we cite. Write it down and ask the cemetery whether it allows that.

Does a Veteran automatically get a grave in a national cemetery?

No. VA publishes who may qualify (a discharge other than dishonorable and other categories, with exceptions). Confirm on VA.gov. That is separate from the burial allowance, which is a payment if other rules are met.

Are planning, prepaying, and insurance the same thing?

No. Planning is the preference note. Prepaying is a contract with a funeral home for specific lines. Life insurance pays cash to the person you named (the beneficiary). That person decides where to spend it, unless the payment is assigned to a provider.

How often should I review the note?

The FTC suggests every few years. Change the paper if you move, if your mind changes, or if the funeral home or cemetery you named no longer exists.

If I prepay and the funeral home closes, what happens?

It depends on your state’s law and how the money was held (trust, assigned insurance, or another form). Ask that in writing before you sign. The prepaid funerals guide walks through those questions. Tell the family so they do not pay again.

When you want the money already set aside

After you know what kind of service you want, you can decide how it will be paid. Whole life insurance is a policy that, if it stays in force, pays a set amount —the death benefit— to the person you named, the beneficiary. The premium is what you pay to keep the policy. Final expense insurance is, in practice, a smaller whole life policy meant for a funeral, cremation, and short debts —not a package at one funeral home.

The cash goes to the beneficiary. That person can pay the funeral home they choose, travel, or a simpler service. If the payment is assigned to a provider, the money may go there directly; ask for those rules in writing. Policy price changes with age, health, tobacco, amount, product, and state.
