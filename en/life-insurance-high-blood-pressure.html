Can you buy final expense insurance if you have high blood pressure?

Often yes. High blood pressure is one of the most common conditions in older adults. The CDC explains that it often has no symptoms and is treated with lifestyle changes and medicine. On a small burial-size life policy, that diagnosis by itself is almost never a reason to skip the health questions and buy a plan that waits two years. What does change the product is when high blood pressure already came with a stroke, a recent heart attack, heart failure, dialysis, or oxygen.

The question How it works What matters Questions Companies Cost

Three facts to start with

  • The first product to quote is usually a level plan: a policy with a short health questionnaire that can pay the full amount from the first covered payment, usually with no two-year wait.
  • The price of that plan follows age, sex, and tobacco. There is no extra charge labeled “high blood pressure.”
  • If there is also a stroke, heart failure, or kidney disease on dialysis, it is no longer “blood pressure only.” Use those guides before assuming the same plan applies.

Do not buy a guaranteed-acceptance plan — no health questions, and about a two-year wait for natural death — just because you take a blood-pressure pill. Quote a plan with health questions first. There is no office exam; there is an honest questionnaire.

The worry people actually have

Families look for this coverage because a funeral, the cemetery, and small debts can fall on relatives. Final expense is permanent life insurance in a small amount, meant for that bill — it does not replace a large income policy.

A diagnosis you already have is often called a pre-existing condition. That label does not, by itself, mean a two-year wait. The fear is usually: “Will they only sell me a plan that waits, or is there a path that can pay in full from the start?” The rest of this page explains that difference before naming companies.

What high blood pressure means for your health

The CDC and NHLBI describe blood pressure as the force of blood against the arteries. It is written as two numbers, such as 120/80. The first is when the heart pushes; the second is between beats. High blood pressure — also called hypertension — is when those readings stay at 130/80 or higher.

It often is not felt. That is why so many people learn about it at a routine checkup. Treatment is usually less salt, more movement, not smoking, and, when a doctor recommends it, medication. Left untreated, high blood pressure can damage the heart, brain, kidneys, and eyes.

An insurer does not treat high blood pressure. It decides whether the history, as it is written and as it appears in prescriptions already filled, fits a product it is willing to issue. It does not ask you to be “cured.”

How life insurance reviews a health history

On final expense the usual path is not an office exam. There is a short questionnaire and, on most products we quote, a review of prescriptions. The NAIC reminds consumers that honest answers matter at claim time: a “no” that should have been “yes” can stall or void a payment.

If those questions can be answered without hitting what that product cannot issue, the plan is usually level: the full amount can apply to a covered natural death from the first payment. If it cannot issue that way, some products pay less or return premiums in the first years. If even that cannot issue, a guaranteed-acceptance plan asks no health questions and waits about two years for natural death.

Term life at larger amounts is a different product: sometimes there are labs or a visit to take vitals, including blood pressure. That is a separate conversation. If the need is income or a mortgage, not burial, see term life with pre-existing conditions.

What helps, and what does not

Answering the questionnaire is usually the advantage: it can open more coverage, a lower price, and a full benefit from the first covered payment. The limitation is that the same questionnaire can send you to a wait or to guaranteed acceptance if there is organ damage, a recent hospital stay, or other diagnoses. Waiting to apply “until the pressure is lower” only raises the age if treatment is already stable.

Usually still a plan with questions

  • High blood pressure treated with medication, with no recent hospital stay for that reason.
  • High cholesterol together with high blood pressure, if no other listed damage.
  • Several blood-pressure pills, if they are for blood pressure and not for heart failure.

Usually changes the conversation

  • A stroke, or a short stroke-like episode (TIA).
  • A recent heart attack, heart failure, or a heart procedure.
  • Dialysis, oxygen, or a diagnosis of pulmonary hypertension — not the same disease as the reading on your arm.

What can change a high-blood-pressure application

Each card starts with the health idea. The right side lists only what we can state for the burial products we quote. If we cannot verify it, we say so.

Blood-pressure medicine

In plain language

A pill to lower blood pressure is a treatment, not a “no” by itself. The CDC and NHLBI describe medication as a usual part of control.

What we can state

  • On the burial products we quote, ordinary blood-pressure treatment is not, by itself, a reason to jump to a no-questions plan.
  • The same pill is sometimes also prescribed for heart failure. That is no longer “blood pressure only.” See the heart-disease guide.
  • Do not stop a prescribed medicine to “look healthier” on the application. Filled prescriptions usually show up in the review.

We do not publish a public pill-by-pill list. Bring the names when you quote. We will not guess what each prescription is for.

Other conditions

In plain language

High blood pressure can stand alone. It can also come with damage to the heart, brain, or kidneys.

What we can state

  • A stroke or TIA is not quoted as “blood pressure only.” See stroke.
  • A recent heart attack or heart failure changes the product. See heart disease.
  • Dialysis or a transplant is a kidney file, not a blood-pressure file. See kidney disease.

Tobacco

In plain language

Smoking or other nicotine usually changes the price. That is not the same as an automatic “no.”

What we can state

  • On these burial products, tobacco usually means a tobacco price if the rest of the answers still qualify.
  • A common window is nicotine in the last year for that higher price. We will not publish a different window for every company here.

High blood pressure and cholesterol together

In plain language

This combination is very common. High cholesterol often has no symptoms either.

What we can state

  • On the burial products we quote, high blood pressure plus cholesterol, with no other listed damage, is typically still a health-question plan — not the first reason for guaranteed acceptance.

Office readings

In plain language

The CDC uses 130/80 as the medical threshold. The NHLBI describes stages and a crisis when numbers reach 180/120 or higher.

What we can state

  • On the burial plans we quote, a nurse usually does not come to the home to take your pressure.
  • There are questions and a prescription review. A larger term policy sometimes does measure blood pressure; that is a different product.

We do not have a published burial table that raises the premium solely because your last office reading was high. We will not invent that table.

Pulmonary hypertension

In plain language

The NHLBI describes pulmonary hypertension as pressure that is too high in the lung arteries. It is not the same disease as the reading taken on your arm.

What we can state

  • Tell us the exact words on the diagnosis. We will not treat it as ordinary high blood pressure.

Companies do not all treat it the same way. We will not claim a universal yes or no.

A hospital stay for very high blood pressure

In plain language

A hypertensive crisis is a medical emergency. The NHLBI says to get care right away at readings in that range.

What we can state

  • Tell us if you were admitted and when. That can change which product fits.

A different Mutual of Omaha product (not the Living Promise burial plan we quote here) asks about hospitalization for high blood pressure in five years. We will not apply that five-year question to every company.

These notes are not a quote. Age, tobacco, build, and a second diagnosis can still change the result.

Frequently asked questions

I take three blood-pressure pills. Will they say no?

The number of pills is not, by itself, a “no.” What matters is what they are for and whether there are hospital stays or organ damage. List the names. We will not guess the reason for each prescription.

Should I stop the medication before I apply?

No. Stopping a blood-pressure drug to “look healthier” is dangerous, and filled prescriptions usually show up in the review. Keep your doctor’s treatment.

Does high blood pressure count as pre-existing?

Yes: it is a diagnosis or treatment that already existed when you apply. On final expense that label does not mean an automatic wait. Many health-question plans accept it when there is no other listed damage.

Is there a home blood-pressure exam?

On these burial products, no. There are questions and a prescription review. A larger term policy sometimes does take vitals; we will not mix those rules here.

Can I buy if I also smoke?

There is usually a tobacco price, not an automatic close, if the rest of the questionnaire qualifies. Mention cigarettes, vaping, a patch, or cigars.

Is the no-questions plan cheaper at my age?

Almost never, at the same age and amount, because the company cannot select by health. Quote the health-question plan first.

I have high blood pressure and high cholesterol. Does that send me to the two-year wait?

Usually no, if those are the only histories. It is still a health-question plan. A stroke, a recent heart attack, or dialysis does change that answer.

My doctor says the pressure is “not controlled.” Does that close the plan that pays in full?

On the burial products we quote, we do not have a public table that turns your last reading into a yes or a no. We will not invent that cutoff. Tell us the readings and the other diagnoses; we will not promise an outcome.
