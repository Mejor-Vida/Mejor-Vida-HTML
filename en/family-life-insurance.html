Life insurance for a family member: you can pay; the other person almost always has to sign

You cannot take out a policy on an adult “in secret.” The NAIC says there must be an insurable interest: a recognized financial loss if that person dies. In practice, a funeral or small debts is enough for a child, a grandchild, or a sibling. The person whose life is covered still has to consent, answer health questions if the plan has them, and sign.

Who Interest Roles Cost How Questions

Three facts to start with

  • There are four roles: insured (whose life the policy covers), owner (controls the contract and any cash value), payer (pays the premium), and beneficiary (receives the check). You can be owner and payer; the relative is the insured.
  • A power of attorney does not replace the insured’s signature at the appointed companies we quote. Mutual of Omaha, on simplified underwriting, will not issue on an application signed only by the person who holds the POA.
  • For a funeral, the usual product is final expense (smaller whole life). Term fits a mortgage or a set number of income years. There is no single burial policy that covers several adults at once.

Who you can cover, in this guide

Each relationship has its own signature and owner rules. Start with the page that matches your relative.

Parents

Adult child as owner and payer. The parent signs and answers health questions.

Grandparents

A grandchild often covers final expenses. The grandparent still takes part in the application.

Siblings

You can pay for a brother’s or sister’s burial. Some companies limit who may own the contract.

Other relatives

Aunts, cousins, or a spouse. Owner lists and beneficiary lists are not the same.

Children and grandchildren

Here the minor is the insured. Signatures change: see the children’s guides. Grandchildren.

Was there already a policy?

If someone has died, do not buy a new one. Look for the one that already existed.

What “insurable interest” means, in one sentence

The NAIC puts it this way: a stranger cannot insure your life. People who usually have an interest are close family; sometimes an employer, a business partner, or a major creditor. “Wanting to help” is not enough. There has to be a financial loss if that person dies — a funeral you would pay, a shared debt, or income they provided.

On final expense of about $10,000 to $25,000, appointed companies rarely ask for a long letter when the insured is a parent, a grandparent, or a sibling and the reason is the funeral. If you ask for $100,000 or more, expect them to ask why that amount.

The four roles, without jargon

Mixing them up is the expensive mistake. Paying the premium does not make you the owner. If you are not the owner, the company can refuse to speak with you.

Insured

The person whose death triggers the payment. They must be in the U.S. when you apply, have an SSN or TIN, and have the mental capacity to sign a contract.

Owner

Who changes beneficiaries, taps cash value, and gets the mail. On Mutual of Omaha simplified life, accepted owners include a spouse, a parent, an adult child, a trust, or a business partner. A sibling is sometimes beneficiary-only, not owner.

Payer

The account the premium leaves. That can be you even if the insured is listed as owner — and then you are paying a contract you do not control.

Beneficiary. Who receives the cash. The funeral home does not collect it unless you name them. Tell that person where the policy is.

Power of attorney does not open this door

The CFPB explains that a power of attorney lets someone act in another person’s name. Life insurers are not required to accept that signature on a new application. Mutual of Omaha writes it down: the person who holds the POA cannot attest to the health history or authorize database checks (MIB, prescriptions). After a policy is in force, a POA can help manage it. That is after issue, not before.

Which product fits, by goal

Do not buy “the cheapest ad.” Buy the contract that lasts as long as the need lasts.

  1. Funeral or small debts. Final expense: simplified whole life, typical amounts $2,000–$50,000. Living Promise Level issues ages 45–85, up to about $50,000. Accendo Level can issue through 89, with a $25,000 cap at ages 76–89. If they qualify, the full benefit can apply from day one.
  2. Health that does not pass the questions. Guaranteed acceptance (for example Corebridge GIWL, generally 50–80, $5,000–$25,000). There is about a two-year wait for non-accidental death: the company usually returns premiums plus contract interest. It costs more per dollar.
  3. A mortgage or income for a set term. Term life. Cheaper per dollar, and it ends. It is not the burial product at age 80. See term life insurance.
  4. Several children at once. There is no “family burial plan” for several adults. There are child or grandchild riders on an adult policy, and separate children’s policies. See children’s life insurance and grandchildren.

How to do this without stalling the application

You can ask for numbers and questions without your relative on the call. The formal application is a later step.

  1. Gather facts, not guesses. Age, height, weight, tobacco, medications, and recent hospital stays. A “no” that should have been “yes” can void a claim.
  2. Choose the product and the owner. Say up front that you want to be owner if you will pay. Do not use an online form meant only for the insured: the owner there is usually that person.
  3. Sign with them. Recorded voice, an email or text link, or paper. Both people take part. Then the first draft starts coverage if the plan is level.

If they will not take part

There is no legal shortcut. You can save, compare a prepaid funeral (a different contract, tied to one provider), or wait until they change their mind. A prepaid plan does not leave the same unrestricted cash a policy does.

Frequently asked questions

Can I buy life insurance without the other person knowing?

Not if they are an adult. You need their consent and signature. The usual exception is a minor: a parent or, on some products, a grandparent can own a child’s policy. That is in the children and grandchildren guides.

Does a power of attorney let me sign for my parents?

Not to issue a new policy at the companies we quote. The CFPB describes what a POA is; Mutual of Omaha will not accept an application signed only by the attorney-in-fact because that person cannot answer the health history.

Is there family burial insurance for several adults?

No. Each adult needs their own policy. A rider can cover children or grandchildren on one adult policy, with smaller amounts and an age cutoff.

What type fits a parent who is 75?

If the goal is a funeral, level final expense if they can still answer the health questions. If they do not qualify, look at guaranteed acceptance. A 10-year term at that age is a different product, and it ends.

Should I be the owner?

If you pay, yes, that is usually wiser. If the insured is the owner, the company will only speak with that person. Cash value on a whole life policy the parent owns can count as a resource in a Medicaid review; that is not legal advice — an elder-law attorney confirms it.

Until what age can a new policy be issued?

It depends on the product. Many final expense plans issue through 85. Accendo Level can issue through 89, with a $25,000 cap at ages 76–89. Appointed GIWL usually stops at 80. We do not publish new final-expense issue at 90.
