Fake letters about “unclaimed life insurance”: how to protect your family

July 15, 2026 | Mejor Vida Insurance | About 10 min read

A letter that looks like it came from a lawyer may promise millions because of a shared last name. It is a known scam. Here is how to spot it and what to do without panic.

Published: July 15, 2026 · Source: see links at the end of this article

Have you ever opened the mail and seen a letter that looked like it came from a lawyer? The letter may say you share a last name with someone who died. It may claim you can split a huge life insurance payout. That story sounds hopeful. For many families, it is also a trap.

This week, local consumer officials repeated a Federal Trade Commission (FTC) warning about these letters. Scammers pretend to be lawyers. They say there is an unclaimed life insurance policy worth millions. They offer to split the money with you, a charity, and their fake firm. The goal is not to help your family. The goal is to steal your information or your money.

If you are shopping for final expense insurance or already own life insurance, this matters. Real policies protect people you love. Fake letters try to use that hope against you. Knowing the difference keeps your family safer.

What happened this week

On July 15, 2026, Orange County officials in Florida posted a fresh scam alert for residents. They pointed back to a Federal Trade Commission consumer warning dated July 9, 2026. The warning describes mailed letters that claim you can collect unclaimed life insurance proceeds. The letters often say the dead person has your same last name. That detail is meant to feel personal and urgent.

According to the FTC, the letters are not from real attorneys. There is no real multi-million-dollar policy waiting for you. If you reply, scammers may ask for your Social Security number. They may ask for bank details. They may ask you to send money for fees. Any of those requests is a red flag.

Real unclaimed property does exist in the United States. States run official programs where people can search for free. You should start on a government website, not a stranger’s letter. For Florida, the alert pointed people to the state treasure hunt site. For other states, usa.gov lists official unclaimed money tools. Those searches do not require secret emails to unknown lawyers.

A clear example

Rosa, age 68 in Omaha, Nebraska, gets a letter that looks official. It says a distant relative left a life insurance benefit of several million dollars. The so-called lawyer asks her to email right away and keep it private. Rosa’s daughter helps her stop. They do not reply. They report the letter at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Rosa keeps her personal data safe. Her real final expense plan for funeral costs stays separate from the scam story.

Why do these scams keep returning? Life insurance feels serious and trustworthy. Final expense insurance and other life policies are real ways families plan for hard days. Scammers borrow that language because it works. They also target people who may not want to ask for help. A private letter can feel safer than a phone call. That privacy is part of the trick.

You can also protect neighbors and parents. Share this warning in your church group or family chat. Many people throw the letter away. Someone else may be ready to reply. A short conversation can stop a costly mistake.

What this means for you

Treat any surprise letter about a stranger’s life insurance payout as fake until proven otherwise. Do not send money. Do not share your Social Security number or bank account. Do not keep it secret from family if you feel unsure. Talk with someone you trust before you reply to anything that promises a huge reward.

Also take one positive step for your own family. Make sure the people you love know about any real final expense insurance or life insurance you already have. Write down the company name, policy number, and who to call. Real coverage helps with funeral costs and family needs. Fake letters only create risk.

Frequently asked questions

Are letters promising unclaimed life insurance real?

Almost never. The FTC warns these letters are usually scams. Do not send money or personal data. Search unclaimed property only on official .gov sites.

What should I do if I get one of these letters?

Do not reply. Talk with someone you trust. Report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Keep real policies separate and tell your family who the beneficiary is.

Does this affect my real final expense policy?

No. A legitimate final expense policy is still valid. The alert is about not confusing a scam with your real coverage.
