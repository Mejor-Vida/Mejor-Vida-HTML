Weekly U.S. Life & Final Expense Insurance Update

July 19, 2026 · News & guides for July 12–18, 2026 | Mejor Vida Insurance | About 6 min read

📰 3 stories | 🛡 Life insurance scams | ⚰ Final expense | ⏱ Term life

Each week we summarize useful insurance news and planning topics so families can protect what matters. This edition covers a warning about fake “unclaimed life insurance” letters, how final expense insurance compares with prepaid funeral contracts, and what to do when a term life policy is ending.

Story 1: Fake letters about “unclaimed life insurance” — how to protect your family

Source: Orange County Government (Florida) / FTC | Published: July 15, 2026

This week, local officials in Florida repeated a warning about mail that looks like it came from a lawyer. The letter often says you share a last name with someone who died and can “split” a huge life insurance payout. The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) says these letters are not from real attorneys. They are scams meant to steal personal information or money.

Why should you care? The words “life insurance” sound trustworthy — especially if you are shopping for final expense coverage or already own a policy. Scammers borrow that trust. Example: Rosa, age 68 in Omaha, gets an “urgent” letter. Her daughter helps her ignore it and report it.

Does this affect you? Yes, if a letter like this arrives at your home or a parent’s home. You do not need to send anything. Real unclaimed property is searched for free on official government sites, not through secret emails to strangers.

Should you do anything? Keep real policies where your family can find them, tell loved ones who the beneficiary is, and treat any “millions for a shared last name” promise as fake. Legitimate coverage is still fine — this alert is about not mixing hope with fraud.

Story 2: Final expense vs prepaid funeral — which protects your family better?

Source: Consumer guide (NFDA / FTC) | Published: July 19, 2026

Many families want one promise: their children will not face a large funeral bill alone. Some people prepay a funeral home. Others buy final expense insurance. They sound similar, but they are not the same. Final expense is a small whole life policy: money goes to your beneficiary. A prepaid contract usually locks services to one business and one package.

Why does it matter? Funerals are not cheap. According to the NFDA, the 2023 national median was $8,300 (burial with viewing) and $6,280 (cremation). Cemetery costs can add thousands more, and prices keep changing. Your local General Price List is the real number.

Does this affect you? If you may move, if your children live in another state, or if plans later shift toward a simpler cremation, insurance often gives more flexibility. The beneficiary can use the money where it is needed. A prepaid contract can be harder to move.

Should you do anything? Before signing a prepaid plan, ask what happens if you relocate or change plans. If you choose final expense, pick a realistic amount and name clear beneficiaries. The goal is to protect your family — not trap them in one path.

Story 3: What happens when your term life policy ends?

Source: Consumer guide (III) | Published: July 19, 2026

Term life insurance often feels affordable when kids are young: you pick 10, 20, or 30 years and a large death benefit. When the term ends, that lower rate and large benefit do not continue on their own. You may renew at a much higher price, convert to permanent coverage if the contract allows it, or simply lose that protection.

Why should you care? Many families discover the end date too late. Ana, age 52 in Denver, bought a 20-year term when her children were teens. When it ends, she may no longer need $500,000 — but she may still want $15,000 to $25,000 of permanent coverage for funeral costs. That is where final expense or whole life can become a “second chapter.”

Does this affect you? If you (or your parents) have term life, check the end date and whether a conversion window is still open. Term life helps replace income; final expense helps with funeral costs. Those are different goals.

Should you do anything? Mark your calendar one year before the end date. Ask for conversion rules in writing and a sample renewal price. A calm review protects your family better than a last-week rush.
