Story 1: Unum Completes $3.8 Billion Long-Term Care Reinsurance Transaction with Fortitude Re

Source: Business Wire / Unum | Published: July 6, 2026

Unum Group announced on July 6, 2026, that it has completed a $3.8 billion long-term care (LTC) reinsurance transaction with Fortitude Reinsurance Company, transferring the bulk of the risk associated with roughly 50,000 legacy LTC policies that Unum has carried on its books for decades. The deal is the latest and largest step in Unum's multi-year strategy to reduce its exposure to a product line that has proven far costlier than actuaries originally projected when many of these policies were written in the 1990s and early 2000s.

Under the terms of the agreement, Unum ceded approximately $3.8 billion in reserves associated with the covered block, with the total value of reserves tied to the transaction expected to grow to roughly $4.5 billion as the policies continue to age and claims develop over time. The structure reflects a growing trend among legacy LTC carriers to offload blocks of business that consume disproportionate amounts of capital relative to the premium revenue they generate.

A notable feature of the transaction is the recapture of a block of policies previously ceded to Fairwind Re, a captive reinsurer that had been holding a portion of Unum's older LTC risk. By recapturing the Fairwind Re block before retroceding it — along with additional in-force business — to Fortitude Re, Unum was able to consolidate its legacy LTC exposure into a single, cleaner transaction rather than leaving the risk fragmented across multiple reinsurance arrangements.

Under the new arrangement, Fortitude Re assumes the biometric risk on the covered policies — meaning the risk tied to policyholder morbidity, mortality, and claims incidence, which has historically been the primary driver of adverse LTC experience across the industry. By retroceding this biometric exposure to a well-capitalized reinsurer specializing in legacy block acquisitions, Unum reduces its own balance-sheet sensitivity to the kind of claims-severity surprises that have plagued LTC carriers industrywide for the past two decades.

Importantly, Unum will retain the administration of the transferred policies. Policyholders will continue to pay premiums to Unum, file claims through Unum's existing claims organization, and interact with the same servicing infrastructure they have used for years. This "retained administration" structure is common in large reinsurance transactions and is designed to minimize disruption for policyholders while still allowing the ceding company to transfer the underlying financial risk off its balance sheet.

The July 6 transaction brings Unum's cumulative long-term care risk transfer activity to more than $7 billion since the company began systematically de-risking its legacy LTC book roughly a decade ago. Company executives have described the LTC line as a "run-off" business — one Unum no longer actively sells but continues to manage responsibly as existing policyholders age into their claims-prone years.

Even after this transaction, Unum retains approximately $11 billion in LTC reserves on its own balance sheet, underscoring the sheer scale of legacy LTC exposure still carried by the company and, by extension, the broader industry. Analysts note that additional risk-transfer transactions are likely in coming years as Unum and its reinsurance partners continue to work through the remaining block.

The transaction reflects a broader industry pattern. Legacy LTC business, largely underwritten before insurers had decades of claims data to properly price the product, has generated persistent losses across the sector due to underestimated claim incidence, longer-than-expected claim durations, and lapse rates far below original pricing assumptions. Rather than continuing to absorb this risk directly, carriers including Unum, Genworth, and others have increasingly turned to specialized reinsurers such as Fortitude Re, which has built a business model around acquiring and managing exactly this type of legacy long-duration risk.

For policyholders, rating agencies, and regulators, transactions of this size are generally viewed as credit-positive for the ceding company, since they reduce capital strain and improve financial flexibility. However, consumer advocates continue to monitor these deals closely to ensure that servicing quality and claims-paying commitments to policyholders remain unaffected by the change in reinsurance backing.

★ What This Means for Agents

  • Reassure LTC policyholders: clients who hold legacy Unum LTC policies should understand that Unum remains their carrier of record and continues to administer claims — the reinsurance change does not affect their coverage, premiums, or benefits.
  • Watch industry-wide de-risking trends: agents working with clients who hold older LTC policies from any carrier should expect more reinsurance and risk-transfer announcements as insurers continue to manage legacy blocks that were mispriced decades ago.
  • LTC pricing lessons for new sales: use this transaction as a talking point when discussing modern hybrid life/LTC and linked-benefit products, which are designed with more conservative, data-driven pricing than legacy stand-alone LTC policies.
  • Monitor carrier financial strength: transactions like this one generally strengthen a carrier's balance sheet, but agents should still track AM Best and other rating agency commentary on any carrier holding significant legacy LTC exposure.

Story 2: Medigap Premiums Surge 12–26% as Seniors Face Sticker Shock on Renewal Notices

Source: CBS News | Published: July 8, 2026

A CBS News report published July 8, 2026, detailed a wave of steep Medigap premium increases hitting seniors across the country, with renewal notices showing rate hikes ranging from 12% to as much as 26% depending on the carrier, plan type, and state. For many older Americans living on fixed incomes, the increases represent one of the largest single-year jumps in Medicare Supplement premiums in recent memory, and they are prompting a fresh wave of client calls to agents nationwide.

Plan G, the most popular Medigap plan sold to newly eligible Medicare beneficiaries since Plans C and F were closed to new enrollees in 2020, has been hit particularly hard. Carriers cite a combination of rising medical costs, higher-than-expected utilization among an aging enrollee base, and several years of claims experience that came in worse than originally priced. Because Medigap plans are guaranteed renewable and community- or issue-age-rated in most states, carriers must periodically true up premiums to reflect actual claims costs rather than being able to selectively adjust rates for individual policyholders.

The premium increases arrive alongside other 2026 Medicare cost adjustments. The Part B annual deductible rose to $283 for 2026, meaning beneficiaries must pay that amount out of pocket for covered services before Medicare Part B begins to share costs. Plan G, which covers the Part B deductible-adjacent cost-sharing but not the deductible itself in its standard form, still leaves beneficiaries responsible for that initial $283 before supplemental coverage benefits fully kick in on many services.

For seniors facing double-digit premium increases, one of the most important tools available is the "birthday rule," now in effect in a growing number of states. Under birthday rule provisions, Medigap policyholders are permitted to switch to a plan with equal or lesser benefits from a different carrier — without medical underwriting — during a window around their birthday each year, typically 30 to 60 days depending on the state. This allows beneficiaries facing a steep rate hike from their current carrier to shop for a more competitively priced Plan G (or other plan type) elsewhere without fear of being declined or rated up for pre-existing conditions.

Not all states have adopted birthday rule or similar guaranteed-issue switching provisions, however, which means many beneficiaries facing large increases have no guaranteed path to switch carriers without full medical underwriting. In states without such protections, a client with health conditions who wants to escape a rate hike may be medically underwritten and could be declined or charged a higher premium by a new carrier — leaving the original, more expensive policy as the only guaranteed option.

The premium surge is also accelerating a longer-term trend of Medigap-eligible seniors considering a switch to Medicare Advantage (MA) plans, many of which advertise $0 premiums. However, the tradeoffs are significant: MA plans use provider networks and often require referrals or prior authorization, while Medigap plans paired with Original Medicare offer broad provider access with no network restrictions. Beneficiaries who switch from Medigap to MA and later want to switch back generally face medical underwriting to re-obtain Medigap coverage, except during specific guaranteed-issue windows — making the decision effectively a one-way door for many seniors with health conditions.

Consumer advocates quoted in the CBS News report urged beneficiaries not to make hasty decisions driven purely by premium sticker shock. They recommend that seniors facing a steep Medigap increase first compare like-for-like plans from other carriers in their state, taking advantage of birthday rule or other guaranteed-issue rights where available, before considering a permanent move to Medicare Advantage that could be difficult or impossible to reverse.

The report also highlighted regional disparities in the size of the increases, with some states seeing average Medigap rate hikes concentrated in the higher end of the 12–26% range due to smaller risk pools and less competitive markets, while more populous states with more carrier competition tended to see increases toward the lower end of the range.

★ What This Means for Agents

  • Proactively review renewal notices: reach out to Medigap clients ahead of their renewal date to review their new premium and compare it against competing Plan G offerings in their state before they receive a surprise bill.
  • Know your state's birthday rule: confirm whether your state offers a birthday rule or annual guaranteed-issue switching window, and calendar each client's window so you can act quickly when a better-priced option becomes available.
  • Explain the Medigap-to-MA tradeoff clearly: before any client switches from Medigap to Medicare Advantage to escape a premium increase, walk through network restrictions, referral requirements, and the likely difficulty of switching back if health changes.
  • Set expectations on the Part B deductible: make sure clients understand that the $283 Part B deductible applies before many Plan G benefits engage, so premium comparisons should be paired with a full explanation of out-of-pocket exposure.

Story 3: NAIFA Issues Life Settlement Alert, Urges Agents to Educate Clients on Tax Tiers and Alternatives

Source: InsuranceNewsNet / NAIFA | Published: July 11, 2026

The National Association of Insurance and Financial Advisors (NAIFA) issued a formal life settlement alert on July 11, 2026, urging member agents to significantly improve how they educate clients — particularly seniors considering surrendering or lapsing a life insurance policy — about the life settlement market, its tax implications, and viable alternatives that may serve clients better in many circumstances. The alert, covered by InsuranceNewsNet, comes amid growing regulatory and consumer-advocate attention to a secondary market that NAIFA says remains poorly understood by both consumers and many producers.

A life settlement occurs when a policyholder sells their life insurance policy to a third-party investor for a lump-sum payment that is typically greater than the policy's cash surrender value but less than its death benefit. The investor then takes over premium payments and becomes the beneficiary, collecting the full death benefit when the insured passes away. For seniors who no longer need or can afford coverage, a life settlement can provide meaningful liquidity that a simple policy lapse or surrender would not.

NAIFA's alert places particular emphasis on the complex, tiered tax treatment of life settlement proceeds — an area the organization says is widely misunderstood by consumers and often glossed over by agents unfamiliar with settlement transactions. Under current IRS guidance, proceeds are generally taxed in three tiers: amounts up to the policyholder's cost basis (total premiums paid) are received tax-free as a return of capital; amounts above basis but up to the policy's cash surrender value are taxed as ordinary income; and any amount received above the cash surrender value — reflecting the settlement premium paid by the investor for the death benefit — is taxed as long-term capital gain. Clients who fail to anticipate this tiered structure can be surprised by a tax bill that meaningfully reduces their net proceeds.

The alert also urges agents to walk clients through accelerated death benefit (ADB) riders before recommending or facilitating a life settlement. Many in-force policies already include ADB riders — often built into the policy at no additional cost or available for a modest premium — that allow policyholders to access a portion of their death benefit while still living if they are diagnosed with a qualifying chronic or terminal illness. In many cases, an ADB claim can provide needed liquidity without permanently severing the policyholder's connection to the remaining death benefit that a full life settlement sale would eliminate.

NAIFA highlighted a particular concern for clients relying on or approaching eligibility for Medicaid long-term care benefits. Life settlement proceeds are counted as an asset for Medicaid eligibility purposes in most states, and a lump-sum payment can push a client over their state's asset limit, potentially triggering a period of Medicaid ineligibility or requiring a spend-down of the proceeds before benefits resume. Agents working with clients who anticipate needing nursing home or long-term care Medicaid benefits in the near term should coordinate closely with an elder law attorney before pursuing a settlement transaction.

The alert also addresses the market realities facing smaller policies. Institutional life settlement buyers generally favor policies with face amounts above $100,000, and often prefer policies well above that threshold, because the fixed transaction and due-diligence costs of a settlement make smaller policies less economically attractive to purchase. Clients holding policies under $100,000 in face amount frequently find few, if any, institutional buyers interested in a traditional life settlement, and may need to explore a viatical settlement (available to terminally ill insureds, typically with a life expectancy of two years or less) or simply retain the policy and use an available ADB rider instead.

NAIFA is calling on its membership to treat life settlement education as a standard part of the policy review process for senior clients holding permanent life insurance, particularly when a client mentions they are considering letting a policy lapse due to affordability concerns. The organization notes that many lapsing policyholders are unaware that a settlement or an ADB claim could provide value that a simple lapse — which forfeits all benefits — cannot.

The alert follows continued state-level regulatory activity around life settlement disclosure requirements, with several states either recently enacting or currently considering legislation that would require insurers to notify policyholders of the existence of the life settlement market before a policy lapses or is surrendered for a reduced value. NAIFA supports enhanced disclosure but emphasizes that agents, not just carriers, bear responsibility for ensuring clients understand their full range of options.

★ What This Means for Agents

  • Make settlement education routine: whenever a senior client mentions lapsing or surrendering a permanent policy, walk through life settlement, viatical settlement, and ADB rider options before any final decision is made.
  • Explain the three-tier tax structure clearly: help clients understand that life settlement proceeds are taxed in tiers — tax-free return of basis, ordinary income up to cash surrender value, and capital gains above that — so they can accurately estimate their net proceeds.
  • Coordinate on Medicaid timing: for clients who may need long-term care Medicaid in the near future, loop in an elder law attorney before a settlement transaction to avoid an unexpected period of benefit ineligibility.
  • Recognize the sub-$100,000 gap: for smaller policies that institutional buyers typically decline, prioritize ADB riders or viatical settlement options rather than assuming a traditional life settlement is available.
