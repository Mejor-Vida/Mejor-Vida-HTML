## Spoken script

### The situation

Hi. I’m Jhenny, the educational assistant for Mejor Vida Insurance.

You may want a small life insurance policy so a funeral is paid if a relative dies who is not your parent, your spouse, or your child — an aunt, an uncle, a cousin, a brother-in-law, a niece, a nephew. That product is often called final expense: a smaller whole-life amount meant for a funeral, not a large term policy.

It feels the same as covering a parent: you would pay the funeral, so you want a policy. On the insurance company’s side, it is two separate questions. The first is whether they will insure that person at all. The second is whether you will be allowed to control the contract.

### Two questions

The first question is insurable interest. The NAIC says you cannot buy a policy on a stranger. There has to be a real financial loss if that person dies, for example a funeral you would pay. Wanting to help is not enough. A friend or a neighbor, with no money at stake, usually does not qualify.

Even when that first question is yes, a second question remains: who is allowed to own the policy. Owning the policy is not the same as paying for it, and it is not the same as receiving the check.

### Three jobs

The insured person is the adult whose death would trigger the payment. That person always has to sign. There is no secret adult policy.

The owner runs the contract: they change who gets paid, and they are who the company will talk to. Someone else can pay the premium. Paying the bill does not make you the owner.

The beneficiary receives the check. A contingent beneficiary is only a backup: they are paid if the first person has already died. That backup is not the owner.

Those three jobs sit on one application, so they sound like one role. They are not. The difference shows up when you call and they will only speak with the owner.

### One company example

Every company writes its own rules. Here is one example. On Mutual of Omaha simplified life, a spouse, a parent, or an adult child can often be the owner. A sibling can often receive the check, but not own the policy. Aunts, uncles, nieces, nephews, and cousins are often only a backup. They are not the owner. Other companies write different rules.

### If you cannot own

If that company will not let you own the policy, there are two honest paths. One: the relative owns it and you pay, knowing the company may not answer you. Two: the policy is not issued, and you keep the money. Do not sign an application in someone else’s name. That can be a crime, and it voids the contract.

When you ask for a quote, say the exact relationship and the insured person’s age. The premium follows their age, not yours.

### Close and quote

So there are two questions. First, whether you have a real financial reason to insure that relative. Second, whether that company will let you own the policy, or only collect, or only be a backup. The adult whose life is covered still has to sign.
